## A Scenario That Made the World Argue

In April 2025, a small team of researchers — including a former OpenAI insider — published **AI 2027**, a month-by-month fictional-but-argued scenario in which AI systems become capable of automating AI research itself, triggering a runaway acceleration that reaches superhuman capability by 2027 and forces a fateful choice between racing ahead and slowing down. It was read by millions, debated in governments, praised as the most rigorous public attempt to think concretely about fast AI progress, and attacked as science fiction with footnotes.

Then something instructive happened: reality weighed in. By late 2025 the authors added a note clarifying that 2027 was their *fastest plausible* path, not their median guess — and in December 2025 they publicly revised their central estimate toward roughly 2030, observing that "things seem to be going somewhat slower" than the scenario depicted.

:::{note} Why we still read it
If the timeline already slipped, why assign it? Because the *reasoning* is the reading. AI 2027 shows its work: explicit assumptions, quantified guesses, named feedback loops (AI speeding up AI research), and identified tripwires to watch for. That makes it checkable — and checking it is exactly the skill this course wants you to build. A vague prediction can never be wrong; a concrete scenario teaches you something even when it's off by three years. *Especially* when it's off by three years.
:::

## Prediction vs. Scenario Planning

The distinction matters beyond AI. A **prediction** says: *this will happen*. A **scenario** says: *here is one coherent way things could unfold, given stated assumptions — watch these indicators to see if we're on this path.* Scenario planning was developed by militaries and oil companies precisely because prediction kept failing them; its purpose is not to be right but to stretch the space of futures you're prepared for.

That reframe changes how you read. The question to ask of AI 2027 is not "will this happen on schedule?" (almost certainly not — nothing does) but:

- **Which assumptions are doing the heavy lifting?** (Here: that AI can substantially accelerate AI research itself)
- **What would count as evidence we're on — or off — this path?**
- **Which parts don't depend on the timeline at all?** (The geopolitics of racing; the difficulty of stopping; the concentration of power)

## Checking Scenarios Against Rulers

Speculation needs a ruler to be measured against, and this week's second reading is the ruler: Stanford's annual **AI Index**, the most-cited attempt to measure what AI systems can actually do, what they cost, who is building them, how fast businesses adopt them, and what the public thinks. Where AI 2027 narrates, the Index counts.

Reading the two together is the whole lesson of this section in miniature:

| Question | The scenario's answer | The ruler's answer |
|----------|----------------------|--------------------|
| How fast are capabilities improving? | Explosively, via self-acceleration | Fast on benchmarks — with real gains and real plateaus, varying by task |
| Is AI doing real economic work? | Imminently transformative | Adoption is broad but uneven; measured productivity effects are early and modest |
| Does the public buy the hype? | Largely a background character | Majorities are more concerned than excited — a gap between builders and everyone else that recurs all semester |

Neither source wins. The scenario asks questions measurement can't ("what if the feedback loop closes?"); the measurement disciplines questions speculation can't ("how would we know?"). Every strong argument you write this semester will pair the two moves.

## The Forecast-Reader's Toolkit

You will meet technology forecasts weekly for the rest of your life — in earnings calls, headlines, policy debates, and job decisions. Five questions cut most of them down to size:

1. **Who benefits if I believe this?** Forecasts are often sales documents — for products, for policies, for panic. (Note: doom can sell as well as hype.)
2. **What's the track record?** Of this forecaster, and of this *kind* of claim. Confident timelines for transformative tech have a century of missing late — and occasionally missing early.
3. **Is it falsifiable?** Does the forecast name indicators that would prove it wrong, and by when? AI 2027 does — which is why it could be revised. "AI will change everything eventually" cannot fail, and therefore cannot inform.
4. **What's measured vs. narrated?** Find the ruler. If there isn't one, you're reading fiction — possibly useful fiction, but fiction.
5. **Which claims survive the timeline being wrong?** The durable insights in most scenarios are structural, not chronological.

:::{tip} Calibration, not cynicism
The goal is not to sneer at forecasts — the people who dismissed every AI prediction in 2019 were as wrong as the people who believed every one in 2023. The goal is *calibration*: holding beliefs with confidence proportional to evidence, updating when the evidence moves, and respecting people (like the AI 2027 authors) who update publicly. That's rarer, and more valuable, than being right.
:::
