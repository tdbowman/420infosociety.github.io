## The Great Transformations

Human societies have reorganized themselves around their dominant resource a small number of times, and each reorganization changed nearly everything: where people lived, what work looked like, who held power, and what counted as wealth.

```{figure} images/great-transformations.svg
:alt: Timeline diagram showing three societal eras. Agrarian society (until roughly 1800) centered on land and muscle, with most people working in fields. Industrial society (roughly 1800 to 1970) centered on machinery and energy, with people working in factories and cities growing. Information society (roughly 1970 onward) centered on information and computation, with work shifting to services, knowledge, and screens. Arrows between the eras are labeled industrial revolution and information revolution.
:width: 100%
:name: fig-great-transformations

Each transformation changed the dominant resource, the typical workplace, and the shape of power, but none of them erased what came before. We still farm and still manufacture; fewer of us do it, and information systems now coordinate it.
```

In an **agrarian society**, the critical resources are land and muscle. Most people work in agriculture, wealth is measured in acres, and power belongs to whoever controls territory.

The **industrial society** that emerged from the late eighteenth century onward reorganized life around machinery and energy. Steam, coal, rail, and the factory system pulled populations into cities, invented the modern working day, and made capital (factories, machines, infrastructure) the thing that mattered. Sociologists spent a century mapping what industrialism did to families, cities, class, and politics.

Then, somewhere in the second half of the twentieth century, the pattern shifted again. Employment in rich countries tilted from making things toward handling information: managing, teaching, researching, marketing, programming, insuring, entertaining. Computing power got cheap, then ubiquitous, then invisible. By the time you were born, the most valuable companies on Earth dealt not in steel or oil but in software, search, chips, and attention. Today the fastest-growing systems in history are ones that generate information themselves.

Daniel Bell famously called what was emerging the **post-industrial society**, one organized around theoretical knowledge, where services outgrow manufacturing and a technical class rises. Manuel Castells later described a **network society**, organized less around places than around flows of information moving through global networks. "Information society" is the umbrella term this course uses for the whole family of ideas.

:::{note} The transformation is uneven
None of this happened everywhere at once, and it still hasn't. Manufacturing didn't disappear; much of it moved. Agrarian life didn't vanish; it was automated and marginalized. And billions of people participate in the information society only partially, a theme (the digital divide, information labor, global inequality) that returns throughout this course.
:::

## Five Ways to Define an Information Society

Saying "information matters now" is easy. Saying precisely *what makes a society an information society* turns out to be hard, and the sociologist Frank Webster showed why: people who use the term are usually measuring five different things.

| Definition | The claim | The evidence it points to |
|------------|-----------|---------------------------|
| **Technological** | New ICTs are so widespread they've changed society's basic operations | Computers, networks, smartphones, now AI everywhere |
| **Economic** | Information industries dominate economic value | Software, media, finance, and data outgrow manufacturing |
| **Occupational** | Most work is now information work | Clerks, coders, analysts, teachers outnumber factory workers |
| **Spatial** | Networks reorganize space and time | Global markets, remote work, instant communication |
| **Cultural** | Everyday life is saturated with media and symbols | Feeds, screens, notifications, influencers, algorithmic culture |

Each definition captures something real, and each has a weakness. Count computers and you've measured boxes, not social change. Count information workers and you have to decide whether a barista with a tablet-based till counts. Measure media saturation and you're describing experience, not structure.

The choice matters because *which definition someone uses determines what they think should be done*. A policymaker working from the economic definition worries about competitiveness and industrial strategy. One working from the cultural definition worries about misinformation and mental health. So when you hear claims about "the digital economy" or "the AI revolution," your first critical move, this semester and for the rest of your life, is to ask: **what exactly is being measured, and by whom?**

## The Skeptics' Question: Continuity or Revolution?

Not everyone agrees that an "information society" is a new kind of society at all. Critics like Webster himself point out that the people who owned the industrial economy largely own the information economy too; that information has *always* been central to markets, states, and war; and that calling the present a revolution conveniently flatters the companies selling it. On this view, we live in *informationalized capitalism*: the same game with faster pieces.

The argument is bigger than academic hair-splitting. It decides between two stories about your own future. If information technology is genuinely revolutionary, then old rules (about work, privacy, monopoly, truth) may simply fail to apply, and we need new ones. If it's continuity, then the oldest questions (who owns what? who benefits? who decides?) are still exactly the right ones, just aimed at new targets.

:::{tip} Hold both ideas at once
The most defensible position, and the one this course takes, is that both stories are partly true. Genuinely new capabilities (instant global communication, machine intelligence) are being deployed inside very old structures (markets, states, inequalities). Most of the interesting questions in this course live in the tension between the two.
:::

## Why This Course, Why Now

The first version of this question, "what did industrialization do to society?", took scholars a century to answer, mostly after the fact. You don't have that luxury. The information society's biggest transformation may be happening *right now*, as artificial intelligence moves from answering questions to doing work. The modules ahead give you the toolkit: how AI and automation actually work and what they do to labor (Modules 3–4, 11–12), how the information society defends and attacks itself (Modules 5, 13), how it decides what's ethical (Module 6), whom to trust (Module 7), and what's true (Modules 8, 10, 14).
