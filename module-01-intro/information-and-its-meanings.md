## The Slipperiest Word in the Course

Odd way to start a course called *The Information Society*, but here it is: the scholars who study information for a living do not agree on what it is. That's less scandalous than it sounds, since physicists still argue about what energy *is* while using it flawlessly. It does matter, though. Different definitions license different conclusions about everything else in this course.

Consider a single example. A weather sensor in a field records `41.2`. Is that information?

- To an engineer, yes: it's a signal, transmitted and stored, and it counts as information whether or not anyone ever looks at it.
- To a farmer deciding whether to irrigate, it *becomes* information the moment it answers a question. Before that, it's just a number.
- To the agribusiness that sells sensor data by subscription, it's information because someone will *pay* for it.

One number; three definitions; three entirely different stories about what makes the information society run.

## Objective, or Situational?

This week's Hjørland reading names the fault line precisely. On the **objective** view, information is a property of things themselves (signals, documents, differences in the world) that exists independently of any observer. This is the view built into engineering: Claude Shannon's information theory, which powers every network you use, deliberately ignores *meaning* entirely and measures only how much uncertainty a signal resolves.

On the **subjective/situational** view, the one Hjørland defends, information is always information *for someone, in some situation*. What informs a radiologist looking at a scan is invisible to the rest of us; what informs you depends on what you already know, need, and are trying to do. Nothing is information in itself; things become informative in use.

| View | Information is... | Its home discipline | Its blind spot |
|------|-------------------|--------------------|----------------|
| **Objective** | Signals, structures, differences in the world | Engineering, computer science | Can't explain relevance or meaning |
| **Subjective/situational** | Whatever informs *this* person in *this* situation | Information science, psychology | Hard to build systems on, since every user is different |

The information society needs both views. The objective view built the infrastructure: bits, bandwidth, storage, and now the token streams of large language models. The situational view explains what the infrastructure can't. It explains why the same "content" educates one reader and bores the next. It explains why misinformation is a property of sentences meeting audiences rather than of sentences alone (Module 8), and why "more information" has never automatically meant "better informed."

:::{note} Thing, process, commodity
Michael Buckland's classic distinction is a useful pocket version: information can mean a *thing* (a document, a file: "the information is on the server"), a *process* (becoming informed: "for your information"), or, increasingly, a *commodity* (something bought, sold, and hoarded). The information society is what happens when the third meaning starts dominating the other two.
:::

## The Field That Studies This

The definitional argument defines a whole discipline. This week's Petras reading surveys how **information science** has struggled to say what it studies (information? documents? people? systems?) and lands on a pragmatic identity: the field that studies how people, institutions, and technologies create, organize, seek, and use recorded information. Its neighboring fields each grab one piece of the puzzle: computer science the systems, communication studies the messages, sociology the people. Information science insists on studying the *interactions* among all three.

That makes it the natural home discipline for this course. Every module ahead is an information-science question wearing a topical costume: cybersecurity is about protecting information *and the trust that makes it usable*; misinformation is about information meeting audiences; AI ethics is about what happens when machines join the ranks of information producers.

## When Machines Start Producing It

Which brings us to the twist your generation gets to live through. Every earlier stage of the information society scaled up the *storage and movement* of information: printing, telegraphy, broadcasting, the internet. Generative AI scales up its *production*. Systems trained on much of the written record now produce fluent text, images, and code on demand: information (on the objective view) manufactured faster than humans can evaluate it (on the situational view).

The definitional debate suddenly has teeth:

- If information is objective signal, then an AI hallucination (confident, fluent, wrong) is information just as much as a textbook is.
- If information is what *validly informs someone in a situation*, then much of what these systems produce is something else: information-shaped output whose status depends entirely on human verification.

Keep the number `41.2` and the hallucination problem in mind all semester. Nearly every controversy we'll meet, from deepfakes and algorithmic feeds to AI companions and automated hiring, is at bottom an argument about whether moving and multiplying *signals* is the same thing as informing *people*.

:::{tip} A habit worth building
When you meet any claim about information this semester ("students today have more information than any generation in history," or "AI democratizes information"), try swapping in each definition and watch the claim change truth-value. It's the fastest critical-thinking trick this course will teach you.
:::
