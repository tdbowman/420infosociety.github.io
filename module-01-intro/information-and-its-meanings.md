## The Slipperiest Word in the Course

Here is an uncomfortable fact to start a course called *The Information Society*: the scholars who study information for a living do not agree on what it is. That's not a scandal — physicists still argue about what energy *is* while using it flawlessly — but it matters, because different definitions license different conclusions about everything else in this course.

Consider a single example. A weather sensor in a field records `41.2`. Is that information?

- To an engineer, yes: it's a signal, transmitted and stored — information regardless of whether anyone ever looks at it.
- To a farmer deciding whether to irrigate, it *becomes* information the moment it answers a question — before that, it's just a number.
- To the agribusiness that sells sensor data by subscription, it's information because someone will *pay* for it.

One number; three definitions; three entirely different stories about what makes the information society run.

## Objective, or Situational?

This week's Hjørland reading names the fault line precisely. On the **objective** view, information is a property of things themselves — signals, documents, differences in the world — existing independently of any observer. This is the view built into engineering: Claude Shannon's information theory, which powers every network you use, deliberately ignores *meaning* entirely and measures only how much uncertainty a signal resolves.

On the **subjective/situational** view — the one Hjørland defends — information is always information *for someone, in some situation*. What informs a radiologist looking at a scan is invisible to the rest of us; what informs you depends on what you already know, need, and are trying to do. Nothing is information in itself; things become informative in use.

| View | Information is... | Its home discipline | Its blind spot |
|------|-------------------|--------------------|----------------|
| **Objective** | Signals, structures, differences in the world | Engineering, computer science | Can't explain relevance or meaning |
| **Subjective/situational** | Whatever informs *this* person in *this* situation | Information science, psychology | Hard to build systems on — every user is different |

Both views are load-bearing in the information society. The objective view built the infrastructure: bits, bandwidth, storage, and now the token streams of large language models. The situational view explains everything the infrastructure can't: why the same "content" educates one person, bores another, and radicalizes a third; why misinformation isn't a property of sentences alone but of sentences meeting audiences (Module 8); why "more information" has never automatically meant "better informed."

:::{note} Thing, process, commodity
Michael Buckland's classic distinction is a useful pocket version: information can mean a *thing* (a document, a file — "the information is on the server"), a *process* (becoming informed — "for your information"), or, increasingly, a *commodity* (something bought, sold, and hoarded). The information society is what happens when the third meaning starts dominating the other two.
:::

## The Field That Studies This

The definitional argument isn't just philosophy — it defines a discipline. This week's Petras reading surveys how **information science** has struggled to say what it studies (information? documents? people? systems?) and lands on a pragmatic identity: the field that studies how people, institutions, and technologies create, organize, seek, and use recorded information. Its neighboring fields each grab one piece of the puzzle — computer science the systems, communication studies the messages, sociology the people — while information science insists on studying the *interactions* among all three.

That makes it the natural home discipline for this course. Every module ahead is an information-science question wearing a topical costume: cybersecurity is about protecting information *and the trust that makes it usable*; misinformation is about information meeting audiences; AI ethics is about what happens when machines join the ranks of information producers.

## Enter the Machines

Which brings us to the twist your generation gets to live through. Every earlier stage of the information society scaled up the *storage and movement* of information: printing, telegraphy, broadcasting, the internet. Generative AI scales up its *production*. Systems trained on much of the written record now produce fluent text, images, and code on demand — information (on the objective view) manufactured faster than humans can evaluate it (on the situational view).

Notice how the definitional debate suddenly has teeth:

- If information is objective signal, then an AI hallucination — confident, fluent, wrong — is information just as much as a textbook is.
- If information is what *validly informs someone in a situation*, then much of what these systems produce is something else: information-shaped output whose status depends entirely on human verification.

Keep the number `41.2` and the hallucination problem in mind all semester. Nearly every controversy we'll meet — deepfakes, algorithmic feeds, AI companions, automated hiring — is at bottom an argument about whether moving and multiplying *signals* is the same thing as informing *people*.

:::{tip} A habit worth building
When you meet any claim about information this semester — "students today have more information than any generation in history," "AI democratizes information" — try swapping in each definition and watch the claim change truth-value. It's the fastest critical-thinking trick this course will teach you.
:::
