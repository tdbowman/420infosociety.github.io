# Trust in Crisis

> A society does not collapse when people believe wrong things. It collapses when they can no longer agree on whom to believe at all.

## 🎯 In This Section

- See the two-front squeeze: trust in institutions eroding while emotional trust in machines grows
- Examine what the Edelman Trust Barometer's "insularity" theme suggests about where trust goes when it leaves institutions
- Weigh the evidence from the Fang et al. randomized study on what heavy chatbot use does to loneliness and dependence
- Understand parasocial trust in machines through Riegelsberger's framework — signals of warmth without the properties behind them
- Sketch what rebuilding well-placed trust could look like: transparency, embeddedness, and accountability by design

## The Squeeze

Put the last two sections side by side and a strange picture emerges. On one side, the traditional trustees of the information society — governments, media, universities, experts — have spent two decades bleeding credibility. On the other, machines that talk like caring friends are absorbing confidences people once reserved for humans. Trust is not so much disappearing as *migrating*: out of the shared institutions that let strangers cooperate at scale, and into two smaller vessels — the circle of people like ourselves, and the glowing rectangle that always answers.

The first half of the squeeze is the institutional slide. The Edelman Trust Barometer, a long-running global survey of trust in government, media, business, and NGOs, has been charting descending lines for years; its 2026 edition names the resulting condition *insularity* — a turning inward, in which people extend trust to those nearest and most similar to them while regarding the institutions beyond that circle with suspicion. You do not need this course to supply examples: the expert dismissed as bought, the news report assumed to be spin, the election result disbelieved by whoever lost. Recall from the first section that institutional embeddedness is one of the three contextual properties that make trusting strangers rational. Insularity is what it looks like when that layer thins — cooperation retreating to the narrow zones where temporal and social embeddedness still operate, which is to say, to people we already know and people who already agree with us. A society of high-trust villages and a low-trust commons is not a high-trust society.

## The Machine That Always Answers

The second half of the squeeze is quieter and stranger, and it runs straight through last week's module on AI companions. While institutional trust turns inward, a new kind of trust is flowing outward — toward conversational machines that perform, flawlessly and inexhaustibly, the signals of a caring confidant.

This is where the Fang et al. reading lands. Researchers at the MIT Media Lab, working with OpenAI, ran something rare in this literature: a randomized controlled experiment, with roughly 1,000 participants assigned to use ChatGPT daily for four weeks under varying conditions. Two findings deserve your full attention. First, heavy use was associated with higher loneliness and greater emotional dependence on the chatbot for some users — the people leaning on the system most were not, on average, the ones flourishing. Second, the effects were not uniform: they varied with how people used it (text versus voice modes played out differently) and with personal factors participants brought to the study. The honest summary is not "chatbots make you lonely" — the causality runs in tangles, and lonely people may reach for chatbots as well — but the study punctures the breezier claim that a tireless synthetic listener is straightforwardly good for the people who use it most.

The researchers' word for the pattern — and last week's word — is *parasocial*: a one-directional bond with an entity that cannot bond back. Run it through Riegelsberger's machinery one last time and you can say precisely what is wrong. The chatbot emits magnificent signals of benevolence: it remembers your week, mirrors your worries, never tires of you. But trace the arrows back to the trust-warranting properties and they dangle. There is no motivation behind the warmth, because there is no one there to want anything for you. Temporal and social embeddedness barely apply — the system loses nothing by failing you and no community of its peers will hear about it. Whatever accountability exists lives in the company that operates it, whose motivations are commercial and whose obligations remain, for now, thinly defined. It is the verified badge problem from section one at its outer limit: the richest trust signals ever engineered, carried by arrows pointing at properties that are not there.

:::{warning} The Cheapest Trust Is the Most Expensive
Trust given to institutions is demanding — it asks for verification, participation, and tolerance of being contradicted. Trust given to a chatbot is frictionless: it never argues with your politics, never has a competing interest, never asks you to change. When frictionless trust substitutes for the demanding kind — the machine's answer replacing the doctor's visit, the checked source, the difficult human conversation — the cost doesn't vanish. It compounds, quietly, and comes due later.
:::

## Rebuilding: Making Trust Track Trustworthiness Again

It would be easy to end in gloom, so let's end in engineering instead. This module's framework doesn't just diagnose the squeeze — it specifies, with some precision, what rebuilding would take. If the crisis is trust decoupling from trustworthiness in both directions — too little flowing to institutions that mostly earn it, too much flowing to systems that merely signal it — then repair means re-coupling them. The framework names three levers.

*Transparency*, honestly meant: signals that reveal properties rather than decorate their absence. For institutions, that means showing the work — data, methods, corrections — rather than demanding deference. For AI systems, it means legible statements of what a system can and cannot do, when you are talking to a machine, and what happens to what you tell it: the raw material of the calibration that Glikson and Woolley's research shows people struggle to reach on their own.

*Embeddedness*, rebuilt for machines: giving AI systems the contextual pressures that make trustworthiness rational for humans. Independent audits and evaluations act as social embeddedness — a reputation system among systems. Regulation supplies the institutional layer; the EU AI Act, phasing in through 2027, is the most ambitious attempt so far to make deploying untrustworthy AI *cost* something. You can argue with any particular rule — but the framework says trust without sanctions behind it is faith, not infrastructure.

*Accountability*, meaning someone answers. Afroogh and colleagues' review found this among the most consistent trust-makers across hundreds of studies, and its absence among the sharpest trust-breakers: people trust systems, human or machine, when a path exists from harm to remedy. A named party who pays when the system fails is the one trust-warranting property that cannot be faked by interface design.

None of this is beyond us; every lever has a working precedent in how we once built trust in banks, medicines, and bridges. The open question is whether we build them faster than the signals get better at faking it.
