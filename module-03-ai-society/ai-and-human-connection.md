## The Third Party in Your Text Thread

Somewhere on your phone, an AI is finishing your sentences. Tap the suggested "Sounds good!" under a text, accept an autocompleted email closing, let a chatbot smooth a paragraph before you hit send — and a conversation you think of as two-person quietly becomes three-party: you, the person you're talking to, and a system trained on billions of other people's messages, nudging yours toward what it predicts comes next.

The previous section examined AI at the scale of attacks and elections. This one zooms in to the scale where most of life actually happens: the message, the reply, the story you write and share. Two of this week's readings are experiments at exactly this scale, and together they complicate every simple story you could tell — AI is ruining communication, AI is improving communication — by showing it doing both at once, on different levels.

## The Smart-Reply Experiments

The Hohenstein reading — a 2023 study in *Scientific Reports* by researchers at Cornell and Stanford — asked a deceptively simple question: what do AI reply suggestions actually do to a human conversation? Across a series of experiments, the team had strangers chat while some participants had access to smart replies, then measured how the conversations went — including in a task where partners had to cooperate.

The headline findings run against the doom narrative. Conversations with smart replies moved faster. Their language tilted more positive — hardly surprising once you notice that suggested replies skew agreeable and upbeat. Using the suggestions was associated with partners rating each other as more cooperative, and cooperation in the task improved. On the surface, the algorithmic third party behaved like a good diplomat: quicker exchanges, warmer tone, smoother collaboration.

Then comes the twist. When people merely *believed* their partner was using AI-generated replies, they evaluated that partner more negatively — regardless of whether the partner actually was. The researchers' data suggest actual AI use wasn't reliably detectable; the damage came from suspicion itself. Call it the suspicion penalty: the assistance helps, but the *attribution* hurts. The benefit of the tool and the social cost of being seen using the tool are two separate quantities, and they point in opposite directions.

If that asymmetry holds as AI writing assistance becomes universal, it predicts a strange equilibrium — everyone using AI, everyone concealing it, and everyone discounting everyone else's warmth on the suspicion that a machine supplied it. "Great to hear from you!" becomes hard to price: sincere sentiment, or default suggestion? The words are identical. What's destabilized is not the message but the inference you can draw from it about the person who sent it.

:::{seealso} The Trust Connection
Why should suspicion of AI use make an identical message feel worth less? Because much of what a message communicates was never in the words — it was in the *cost* of producing them: the time, attention, and effort a sender spent on you. Automation collapses that cost, and with it the signal. This is a trust problem as much as a technology problem, and Module 7 takes it up directly, asking what happens to the mechanics of trust when we can no longer tell people and machines apart.

## Everyone Gets More Creative, Everything Gets More Alike

The second experiment scales the question from replies to creativity itself. In the Doshi and Hauser reading, published in *Science Advances* in 2024, hundreds of participants wrote short stories; some wrote unaided, while others could request AI-generated story ideas to build on. Independent readers then judged the stories.

The individual-level result is an advertisement for the technology. Writers with access to AI ideas produced stories judged more creative, better written, and more enjoyable — and the boost was largest for the *less* creative writers (as measured beforehand), whose access to AI ideas lifted their stories dramatically. AI worked as a creativity equalizer, raising the floor and narrowing the gap between stronger and weaker writers. If the story ended there, the conclusion would be simple: give everyone the assistant.

But Doshi and Hauser also measured something an individual writer never sees: the similarity *between* stories. Stories written with AI assistance resembled each other significantly more than the unaided stories did. Every writer drew inspiration from the same well — the same model, with the same tendencies — and their outputs converged. Each individual got better; the collection got blander. The authors frame it as a social dilemma, and it's worth stating precisely: every rational individual choice (use the tool — it demonstrably helps *you*) aggregates into a collective loss (a narrower, more homogeneous pool of stories) that no individual chose and no individual can fix by abstaining.

You have met this structure before — it's the logic of traffic jams and overfished seas, now operating on culture. And you can see how it might generalize: the same convergence pressure applies to every domain where millions of people draw from a handful of similar models — college essays, cover letters, product designs, melodies, jokes. Whether it *does* generalize is an open empirical question. But the experiment gives the course's skeptical toolkit a new blade: when someone shows you that AI improves individual performance, ask what's happening to the distribution.

## Authenticity, Rewritten

Put the two experiments side by side and a common pattern emerges. In both, the AI genuinely helps the individual in the interaction — warmer replies, better stories. In both, something is lost at the level of the *relationship between* people: trustworthy signals in one case, diversity of expression in the other. The harms are real but diffuse, statistical, and nearly invisible from inside any single interaction — which is exactly why they're worth learning to see.

None of this settles what you should do, and honesty requires noticing what the pattern doesn't say. Ghostwriters, greeting cards, and form letters are ancient; nobody thinks a sympathy card is inauthentic because someone else composed the verse. For some people — writing in a second language, or facing a socially demanding message — AI assistance may make communication not less authentic but finally possible, an equalizing effect that echoes Doshi and Hauser's floor-raising result. The honest questions are narrower and harder: which messages carry signals that matter (a condolence note and a pizza order are not the same), what do we owe each other in disclosure, and what norms should a world of universal assistance adopt? Those are the questions to bring to Friday's discussion — along with your own receipts, because nearly everyone in the room has already tapped "Sounds good!"

There is one more step down this road. This module examined AI *mediating* human relationships. A fast-growing industry now offers AI as the relationship itself — companion apps marketed as friends, confidants, and partners. Everything you learned here (the positivity tilt of machine-generated language, the invisible convergence, the suspicion penalty's strange absence when there's no human left to suspect) becomes urgently relevant there. That is Module 6's territory, in the wider context of information ethics.

This module gave you a map of what AI actually is, a framework for its double-edged capabilities, survey evidence on who fears and who welcomes it, and experimental evidence on what it's already doing inside human connection.
