# From Principles to Regulation

> A principle that costs nothing to violate is not a rule. It is a suggestion with good typography.

## 🎯 In This Section

- Follow AI ethics on its journey from voluntary principles to binding law
- Understand UNESCO's 2021 Recommendation: the first global AI ethics standard, adopted by 193 states — and binding on none of them
- Learn the EU AI Act's risk pyramid: what gets banned, what gets regulated hard, what merely has to disclose itself, and what is left alone
- Contrast Europe's approach with the United States' lighter-touch, executive-order-driven path
- Confront the enforcement question: do principles change behavior without teeth?

## The Age of Principles

By the late 2010s, AI ethics had become a genre. Companies, governments, universities, and standards bodies published principle sets by the dozen, and they converged on a familiar chorus: fairness, transparency, accountability, privacy, human oversight. If words alone governed, the problem would have been solved twice over by 2020.

The most significant document of this era is this week's UNESCO reading: the *Recommendation on the Ethics of Artificial Intelligence*, adopted in 2021 by all 193 UNESCO member states — the first global standard-setting instrument on AI ethics, and to date the only one with a genuine claim to speak for nearly everyone. Its structure repays the close look you're giving it this week. The **Values** section names what ultimately matters: human rights and human dignity; environmental and ecosystem flourishing; diversity and inclusiveness; peaceful and just societies. The **Principles** section translates those values into commitments you will recognize from this module — proportionality, safety, fairness and non-discrimination, transparency and explainability, responsibility and accountability, privacy, human oversight. Read them with last week's toolkit in hand and you can see the frameworks' fingerprints everywhere: dignity is deontology's vocabulary, flourishing is virtue ethics', fairness carries Rawls.

But notice the document's grammar: it is a *Recommendation*. No state is bound by it; no company can be fined under it. Its power is the softer kind — a shared reference point that gives regulators, journalists, and citizens in 193 countries the same vocabulary for demanding better. Whether soft power of that kind moves anything is this section's closing question. First, the place where ethics acquired teeth.

## Europe Writes It Into Law

The European Union's **AI Act** — covered in this week's European Parliament explainer — is the world's first comprehensive, binding law regulating artificial intelligence, with provisions phasing in across 2025–2027. Its central design choice is the one to remember: the Act does not regulate "AI" as a single thing. It regulates *uses* of AI according to the risk they pose to people's safety and rights, sorting every system into one of four tiers.

At the top sits **unacceptable risk**: uses judged incompatible with fundamental rights and banned outright — the canonical example is government social scoring of citizens, alongside systems that manipulate people or exploit their vulnerabilities to cause harm. Below that, **high risk**: systems allowed on the market only under strict obligations — risk management, quality training data, documentation, human oversight — because they make consequential decisions about people's lives in domains like education, credit, essential services, and employment. Pause on that last word: the hiring algorithm we have carried through this whole module is not a thought experiment in Brussels. CV-screening software is a textbook high-risk system under the Act, which means everything the frameworks demanded in section one — oversight, documentation, contestability — stops being an ethics seminar's wish list and becomes a compliance checklist. The third tier, **limited risk**, carries transparency duties: chatbots must disclose that they are AI, and AI-generated content must be identifiable as such — a direct response to the impersonation problems in Hagendorff's map. And the wide base of the pyramid, **minimal risk** — spam filters, AI opponents in video games, the overwhelming majority of systems — faces no new rules at all.

Two things make this more than European trivia for an American classroom. First, scale: any company that wants access to the EU market must comply, so the Act shapes products worldwide — you will likely feel its transparency rules in software that never crosses the Atlantic on paper. Second, the pyramid itself is an ethical argument rendered as law: it asserts that some applications are wrong *per se* (a deontological ban no cost-benefit analysis can override), while others are matters of managed consequences (a consequentialist's regime of audits and documentation). The frameworks from section one didn't stay in the seminar room; they got drafted into legislation.

## The American Alternative

The United States has taken a different road: no comprehensive federal AI statute, but a lighter-touch approach steered largely through **executive orders** — presidential directives that guide federal agencies without requiring an act of Congress. The pattern has been for each administration to set AI policy by executive action, which means policy can swing sharply when the White House changes hands: what one administration frames as essential safeguards, the next can frame as barriers to innovation, and executive orders can be revoked with a signature. The current Trump administration has continued the executive-action pattern with an explicit emphasis on American AI leadership and competitiveness; we will examine its AI Action Plan in detail in Module 11, where the course turns to information policy.

For now, hold the structural contrast rather than the partisan one. Europe writes obligations that bind companies directly and persist across elections; Washington writes directions to its own agencies that can be rewritten overnight, leaving most private-sector AI governed by market pressure, existing sector-by-sector law, and the companies' own principles. Neither approach is self-evidently right — binding rules can ossify around yesterday's technology, and flexibility can be another word for nobody's-in-charge — but the two models answer this module's core question about power very differently. Ask of each: who can be punished, by whom, for what?

## Do Principles Have Teeth?

Which brings us to the question this module has been circling since its title. Suppose a company violates UNESCO's principles: nothing happens. Suppose it violates the AI Act: it faces regulators who can fine it a meaningful share of its global revenue. If only the second changes behavior, were the twenty years of principles a waste?

The skeptic's case is strong. Critics coined the term **ethics washing** for the pattern: publish principles, hire an ethics board, sponsor a conference — and change no product decisions that matter. Principles without enforcement can even be counterproductive, functioning as evidence that regulation is unnecessary ("we're handling it"). On this view, voluntary ethics is what industries do to *delay* law.

The defense is subtler. Non-binding instruments do slow, unglamorous work: they build shared vocabulary, train a generation (including you, this week), and stake out consensus that later hardens into law — the AI Act's principles would read as recognizably UNESCO-flavored even without any direct lineage, because both draw on the same converged consensus. Soft law is how the ground gets prepared. The honest answer is probably sequential: principles without eventual enforcement decay into public relations, and enforcement without prior principled consensus has nothing coherent to enforce. The journey this module has traced — frameworks, to issue maps, to declarations, to a risk pyramid with fines attached — is what it looks like when that sequence works. Whether it worked *fast enough*, and for *whose* benefit, is a question you now have the tools to argue about.
