# Module 2: Foundational Theories in Information Science & Informatics 🧩

**Week 2 | From Shannon to Semantics**

## Welcome

Last week you wrestled with what an "information society" is. This week we ask the question that quietly shapes everything else in this course: what is *information* itself? You'll meet the engineer whose answer changed the world — and the philosophers, semioticians, and information scientists who have spent seventy-five years arguing that his answer left out the most important part.

### 🎯 What You'll Learn

- How Claude Shannon's 1948 mathematical theory turned information into something you can measure, and why that made the digital world possible
- Why Shannon deliberately excluded *meaning* from his theory — and why that exclusion still haunts every search engine, recommendation system, and chatbot you use
- The major rival theories of information — semantic, semiotic, and social/domain-based — and what each one gets right
- Why information scientists disagree at several different levels (terminology, theory, metatheory), following Hjørland's framework
- What "knowing" something even means when generative AI can produce fluent, confident text about anything, instantly

### 🧠 Big Questions to Consider

- Can you measure information without caring whether it's true?
- If a signal reaches you perfectly but you misunderstand it, was information transmitted?
- Who decides what counts as information — engineers, philosophers, or communities of practice?
- When ChatGPT writes a paragraph, is it communicating information, generating signals, or something else entirely?
- Is it possible to know more facts than any generation in history and still understand less?

## This Week's Journey

We move in three steps. First, we sit with Shannon's theory long enough to appreciate its genius: information as reduced uncertainty, measured in bits, engineered to survive noise. Second, we tour the counter-tradition — thinkers who insist that meaning lives in interpreters and communities. Third, we bring the debate into the present: what happens to knowledge itself when machines generate humanlike text at essentially zero cost?

### 📚 Core Readings

| Reading | Source | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| [Theory and metatheory in the nature of information](https://doi.org/10.3390/info16090791) | Tredinnick, L. (2025). *Information, 16*(9), 791. | Open access. Your map of the whole territory: Shannon's mathematical theory, Floridi's semantic information, Burgin's general theory, and semiotic approaches, all in one survey. |
| [Information science and its core concepts: Levels of disagreement](https://researchprofiles.ku.dk/da/publications/7647cd26-840c-473b-9e9f-8a4a38abe7d3) | Hjørland, B. (2014). | Open copy via the University of Copenhagen. Explains *why* the field can't agree on its own core concept — the disagreements run deeper than definitions. |
| [Knowing you know nothing in the age of generative AI](https://doi.org/10.1057/s41599-025-04731-0) | Bentley, S. V. (2025). *Humanities & Social Sciences Communications, 12*, 409. | Open access. Traces how knowledge has been passed down from oral culture to generative AI, and argues for a revival of Socratic scrutiny. |

### 🤔 Make You Think (Optional)

- Brier, S. (2014). The transdisciplinary view of information theory from a cybersemiotic perspective. PDF on Canvas — for the philosophically adventurous: what happens when you take signs, life, and consciousness seriously as parts of information theory.
- Hofkirchner, W. (2014). Epistemology and the study of social information within the perspective of a unified theory of information. PDF on Canvas — an ambitious attempt to unify all the perspectives you'll meet this week.
- Khan Academy, [*Journey into Information Theory*](https://www.youtube.com/playlist?list=PLSQl0a2vh4HC9lvrBhVt4UUkhzpp3N5_x) (video playlist) — a friendly visual walk through Shannon's ideas, from smoke signals to entropy. Excellent if the math-flavored parts of this module feel abstract.
