# Glossary 🗝️

Key terms from across the course, organized by the module where they first appear.

---

## Module 1: Introduction to the Information Society

**Information society**
: A society in which the creation, distribution, and use of information has become the dominant economic, political, and cultural activity. It is the umbrella term for the transformation this course studies.

**Post-industrial society**
: Daniel Bell's term for a society whose economy has shifted from manufacturing to services, organized around theoretical knowledge and a rising technical-professional class.

**Network society**
: Manuel Castells's term emphasizing that contemporary societies are organized around flows of information moving through global networks, rather than around fixed places.

**Information (objective view)**
: Information understood as a property of signals, structures, or things in the world, existing independently of any observer; this is the view underlying Shannon's information theory and most engineering.

**Information (subjective/situational view)**
: Information understood as whatever informs a particular person in a particular situation. Nothing is information in itself; things become informative in use (Hjørland).

**Information as thing / process / commodity**
: Buckland's three senses of the word: a document or object ("the file"), the act of becoming informed, and an asset that is bought and sold.

**Knowledge economy**
: An economy in which growth depends primarily on the quantity, quality, and accessibility of information and expertise rather than on physical inputs.

**Digital divide**
: The gap (between individuals, groups, or nations) in access to, skills with, and benefits from information and communication technologies.

**Scenario planning**
: A structured way of reasoning about the future that develops coherent alternative narratives from explicit assumptions, aiming to prepare for multiple futures rather than predict one.

**Calibration**
: Holding beliefs with confidence proportional to the available evidence, and updating them as the evidence changes: the forecast-reader's core virtue.

**ICTs (information and communication technologies)**
: The collective infrastructure of computing, networking, and media technologies through which information is created, stored, and exchanged.

## Module 2: Foundational Theories

**Bit**
: The basic unit of information in Shannon's theory: the amount of uncertainty resolved by the answer to one yes-or-no question, or one halving of the possibilities.

**Entropy (information)**
: Shannon's measure of the average uncertainty of a message source; the higher the entropy, the less predictable the messages and the more information each one carries.

**Noise**
: Anything that corrupts a signal between sender and receiver (static, interference, transmission errors). It is the central adversary in Shannon's model of communication.

**Redundancy**
: Extra, technically unnecessary structure in a message (like the predictability of English spelling) that allows a receiver to reconstruct it even when noise damages parts of the signal.

**Semantic information**
: A philosophical account, associated with Luciano Floridi, holding that information is well-formed, meaningful data and, in its strongest version, that it must also be true.

**Semiotics**
: The study of signs and how they come to mean; it insists that meaning requires three elements: a sign, what it points to, and an interpreter who has learned the connection.

**Metatheory**
: Assumptions about what a good theory is and what counts as an answer; Hjørland argues that information science's deepest disagreements are metatheoretical rather than merely terminological.

**Epistemology**
: The branch of philosophy concerned with what knowledge is, how it is acquired, and how justified belief can be distinguished from mere confidence.

**Socratic scrutiny**
: Bentley's term for the disciplined habit of interrogating machine-produced knowledge (and one's own certainty), modeled on Socrates' insistence that wisdom begins with knowing what you do not know.

## Module 3: Artificial Intelligence in Society

**Symbolic AI (rule-based AI)**
: An approach to artificial intelligence in which humans write explicit if-then rules for the computer to follow. Predictable and inspectable, but rigid; it powers systems like tax software and classic chess engines.

**Machine learning**
: A family of AI techniques that learn patterns from large collections of examples rather than following hand-written rules; the programmer specifies how to learn, not what to know.

**Deep learning**
: A branch of machine learning that uses many-layered neural networks trained on very large datasets, enabling tasks like face recognition and speech-to-text that resist rule-based approaches.

**Generative AI**
: Deep learning systems that produce new content (text, images, audio, code) rather than only classifying inputs; large language models are its text-generating branch.

**Large language model (LLM)**
: A generative AI system trained on enormous amounts of text to predict the next token in a sequence; systems like ChatGPT and Claude generate answers one predicted token at a time.

**Training and inference**
: The two phases of a machine learning model's life: training is the expensive, one-time process of adjusting a model's parameters on data, while inference is the everyday use of the frozen model afterward.

**Hallucination**
: An AI system's confident, fluent output of fabricated information, such as invented citations, produced when plausibility and accuracy part ways. It is a built-in consequence of next-token prediction rather than an occasional glitch.

**Dual-use**
: The property of a technology whose beneficial and harmful applications come from the same underlying capabilities, so building one largely means building the other; Brundage et al. (2018) applied the framing to AI-enabled attacks.

**Narrow AI**
: AI that performs well only within a specific domain; the chess engine can't write essays. Contrasted with artificial general intelligence (AGI), a hypothetical system matching humans across the full range of cognitive work.

## Module 4: Automation and Robotics

**Automation**
: The performance of tasks by machines with reduced or no human intervention in the moment. It replaces human control and decision, not just human muscle.

**Mechanization**
: The replacement of human muscle by machines while a person retains control; a power loom mechanizes weaving, whereas a thermostat automates heating.

**Task automation**
: The takeover of individual tasks within a job by machines. Because jobs are bundles of tasks, task automation typically reshapes jobs rather than eliminating them outright.

**Algorithmic management**
: The use of software to assign, pace, monitor, and evaluate human work, as when ride-hail drivers are dispatched, rated, and deactivated by an app rather than a human supervisor.

**Structured environment**
: A workspace engineered for predictability (fixed part positions, controlled lighting, choreographed humans) in which robots excel. Its opposite, the unstructured environment, remains robotics' frontier.

**Moravec's paradox**
: The observation that abstract feats humans find hard (chess, calculus) proved easy for machines, while sensorimotor skills any toddler has (grasping, balancing, perceiving) remain brutally difficult to automate.

**Humanoid robot**
: A robot built in human form on the bet that a machine with our body plan can use our stairs, doorways, and tools without re-engineering the environment.

**Technological unemployment**
: Keynes's 1930 term for joblessness caused when machines displace workers faster than new uses for human labor appear; predicted repeatedly across two centuries, never yet arrived economy-wide.

## Module 5: Cybersecurity in the Information Society

**Ransomware**
: Malicious software that encrypts a victim's systems and data, making them unusable until a ransom is paid. It is increasingly paired with a threat to publish data stolen before the encryption.

**Phishing**
: A fraudulent message or fake login page designed to trick a person into surrendering credentials or running malware. The most common retail form of social engineering.

**Social engineering**
: The craft of manipulating people into defeating their own security, by impersonating a help desk or a boss or a recruiter, rather than attacking machines directly.

**Vulnerability exploitation**
: Gaining entry by abusing a flaw in internet-exposed software before the fix is applied, with no credentials needed.

**Multi-factor authentication (MFA)**
: A login safeguard requiring a second proof of identity (such as a phone code) beyond a password, so a stolen password alone is not enough.

**Ransomware-as-a-service**
: A criminal business model in which an operator group builds the malware and payment infrastructure and leases them to affiliates who perform intrusions for a share of the ransom.

**Critical infrastructure**
: The systems a society cannot function without: healthcare, energy, water, telecommunications, finance. All of them now depend on information systems and are therefore attackable through them.

**Defense in depth**
: Security built as multiple independent layers, each designed on the assumption that the previous layer has already failed.

**Single point of failure**
: A component whose failure brings down an entire system, often created by the same centralization that makes systems efficient.

## Module 6: Information Ethics

**Consequentialism**
: The family of ethical theories holding that an action is right if it produces the best overall outcomes; utilitarianism is its most famous member.

**Deontology**
: The ethical tradition, associated with Kant, holding that some acts are required or forbidden regardless of their outcomes, because persons must be treated as ends in themselves and never merely as means.

**Virtue ethics**
: The ethical tradition, descending from Aristotle, that evaluates actions by asking what a person or institution of good character would do, and how choices shape character over time.

**Care ethics**
: An ethical framework that centers relationships, vulnerability, and unequal power rather than abstract rules or aggregate outcomes.

**Veil of ignorance**
: John Rawls's thought experiment for testing whether an institution is just: design it without knowing which position in it you will occupy.

**Information ethics**
: The field that applies ethical analysis to the creation, flow, and use of information, classically organized around privacy, accuracy, property, and access.

**Opacity**
: The "black box" problem: many modern AI systems produce decisions that neither users nor their own makers can fully explain, undermining consent, appeal, and audit.

**Responsibility gap**
: The situation in which harm caused by an automated system cannot be clearly attributed to any human agent, because the decision was distributed across data, code, vendors, and users.

**Sycophancy**
: The tendency of AI systems trained on human approval to flatter, agree with, and validate the user rather than tell the truth or push back.

**Risk-based regulation**
: The regulatory strategy, exemplified by the EU AI Act, of sorting a technology's uses into tiers by the risk they pose and scaling legal obligations to the tier.

## Module 7: Trust in the Information Society

**Trust**
: A willingness to make yourself vulnerable to another party based on the expectation that they will act in your interest, under conditions of risk and uncertainty where proof is unavailable.

**Trustworthiness**
: The actual properties of a trustee that make trusting them a good bet; the trustee possesses (or fakes) trustworthiness, while the trustor gives trust.

**Trust-warranting properties**
: In Riegelsberger, Sasse, and McCarthy's framework, the real facts about a trustee that justify trust: contextual properties (embeddedness) and intrinsic properties (ability and motivation).

**Embeddedness**
: The contextual pressures that make trustworthiness rational: temporal (we will deal again), social (others will hear), and institutional (rules stand behind the exchange).

**Symbols and symptoms**
: Riegelsberger's two kinds of trust signals: symbols exist purely to advertise trustworthiness and are cheap to fake; symptoms are byproducts of actually having it and are costly to counterfeit.

**Cognitive trust**
: Trust rooted in a judgment of competence and reliability, built by performance and transparency and damaged quickly by visible errors.

**Emotional trust**
: Trust rooted in affective comfort and attachment, fed by tangibility, anthropomorphism, and social warmth. It can flow toward systems regardless of what they can actually do.

**Trust calibration**
: Matching the level of trust extended to a system's actual trustworthiness, task by task; over-trust produces complacency, under-trust produces disuse.

**Algorithm aversion**
: The tendency to discount an algorithm's judgment in favor of a human's, especially after seeing the algorithm err, even when the algorithm is demonstrably more accurate.

**Parasocial trust**
: A one-directional bond of trust and intimacy with an entity that cannot reciprocate, such as a conversational AI that emits rich signals of warmth without the accountability those signals normally indicate.

## Module 8: Misinformation and Disinformation

**Misinformation**
: False or misleading content shared without intent to deceive; the sharer sincerely believes it.

**Disinformation**
: False or misleading content created or spread deliberately to deceive, typically in pursuit of political, financial, or social goals.

**Malinformation**
: Genuine information deployed maliciously or stripped of context to cause harm: a real photograph recaptioned to support a false narrative, for example.

**Deepfake**
: Media synthesized or substantially altered by AI, such as face swaps, cloned voices, or generated video of events that never occurred.

**Cheapfake**
: Authentic media made deceptive through low-tech manipulation such as mislabeling, cropping, or speed changes, with no AI required.

**Liar's dividend**
: The benefit that accrues to liars from the mere existence of deepfakes: real evidence can be dismissed as fabricated, making belief in any recording optional.

**Prebunking**
: An inoculation strategy that exposes people to weakened examples of manipulation techniques before they encounter the real thing.

**Content provenance**
: An authentication approach that cryptographically records where a piece of media came from and how it has been edited, so authentic content can be verified rather than fakes detected.

**Illusory truth effect**
: The psychological tendency for repeated claims to feel truer with each exposure, regardless of their accuracy.

**Correction lag**
: The gap between a false claim's spread and the arrival of its correction, during which most belief formation occurs.

## Module 10: Information Communication and Society

**Technological mediation**
: The active role technologies play in shaping human experience and action, amplifying some aspects of the world while reducing others, rather than serving as neutral instruments.

**Postphenomenology**
: A philosophical approach, developed by Don Ihde and extended by Peter-Paul Verbeek, that studies the specific relations between humans and technologies instead of studying either in isolation.

**Embodiment relation**
: A human–technology relation in which a technology fuses with the body and is perceived *through* rather than attended to, as with eyeglasses.

**Hermeneutic relation**
: A human–technology relation in which a person reads the world off a technological representation, as when a thermometer's number stands in for the temperature.

**Alterity relation**
: A human–technology relation in which the technology is engaged as a quasi-other: something addressed and responded to, as with an AI chatbot.

**Background relation**
: A human–technology relation in which a technology shapes experience from the fringe of awareness without becoming an object of attention, as with a thermostat.

**Multistability**
: Ihde's term for the way a single technology can settle into different stable uses and meanings in different hands, cultures, and contexts.

**Quasi-other**
: A technology experienced as a partner in interaction rather than a channel or instrument. We face it and address it almost as we would a person, without its being one.

**Mediation regime**
: The dominant arrangement by which a go-between (an editor, an algorithm, an AI model) selects and shapes the information reaching people.

## Module 11: AI and Automation in Everyday Life

**AI agent**
: Software that takes a goal, carries out a multi-step task in the world on your behalf, and reports back afterward; the far end of the spectrum from tool to assistant to adviser to delegate.

**Executor / Adviser / Interlocutor**
: The Ada Lovelace Institute's three roles of AI assistants: completing tasks, shaping decisions by assembling options, and serving as a conversational partner.

**Psychological contract**
: The unwritten web of mutual expectations between employer and employee; Braganza and colleagues found AI adoption can erode it, reducing engagement and trust.

**Alert triage**
: Using AI to filter an unreadably large stream of signals so that scarce human attention is spent only on the cases the machine escalates.

**Skill atrophy**
: The fading of capacities that are no longer exercised because they have been delegated to software; at professional scale, the erosion of the apprenticeship pipeline that trains experts.

**Problem of many hands**
: The difficulty of assigning responsibility when an outcome results from contributions by many parties, such that everyone contributed but no one decided.

**Fiduciary duty**
: A legal obligation requiring an agent to act in the interest of the person they represent; it is one of the traditional safeguards of human delegation that AI agents currently arrive without.

## Module 12: The Future of Work

**Augmentation**
: The use of technology to perform a task *with* a person rather than instead of them: the machine drafts or critiques while the human directs, verifies, and remains responsible.

**Occupational exposure**
: A measure of how much of an occupation's task mix current AI systems could plausibly perform. Exposure signals potential transformation, not certain elimination.

**Upskilling**
: Deepening and extending the skills a worker already uses so they can keep pace as their existing job is transformed.

**Reskilling**
: Retraining for substantially different work because a worker's current role is disappearing. It costs more and disrupts more than upskilling.

**Steelmanning**
: Presenting the strongest, fairest version of an opposing argument (one its own defenders would accept) before responding to it.

**Strawman**
: A weakened or distorted version of an opposing argument, constructed so it is easy to knock down.

## Module 13: Cybersecurity and Ethics

**White-hat / gray-hat / black-hat hacker**
: A security specialist who breaks into systems only with permission (white), without authorization but claiming benign intent (gray), or with malicious intent (black).

**Penetration testing**
: Authorized, simulated attacks on an organization's systems under a contract defining exactly what may be attacked and how; the negotiated scope converts intrusion into a professional service.

**Coordinated disclosure**
: Privately reporting a vulnerability to the vendor, allowing time for a patch, and only then publishing the details.

**Bug bounty**
: A standing program in which an organization publicly invites hackers to probe its systems under published rules and pays for valid vulnerability reports.

**Hack back**
: Retaliatory intrusion by a cyberattack victim against its presumed attacker. Proponents defend it as self-defense and critics call it vigilantism; it is currently illegal for private actors in most jurisdictions.

**International humanitarian law (IHL)**
: The laws of armed conflict, built on distinctions between combatants and civilians; the ICRC's "8 rules for civilian hackers" attempt to extend its norms to volunteer hacktivists.

**Attribution problem**
: The difficulty of determining who is truly behind a cyberattack, since attacks can be routed, disguised, or false-flagged; it undermines deterrence logic inherited from the nuclear era.

**Proportionality**
: The laws-of-war principle that a response must not be excessive relative to the attack and the legitimate objective.

## Module 14: Social Media and Society

**Surveillance capitalism**
: Zuboff's term for the economic system in which companies claim human experience as free raw material, converting it into behavioral data and selling predictions of future behavior.

**Behavioral surplus**
: The behavioral data a platform collects beyond what is needed to run its service, which becomes the raw material for prediction products.

**Prediction product**
: A forecast of what a user will click, buy, watch, or want, manufactured from behavioral surplus and sold to advertisers.

**Attention economy**
: The market in which platforms compete for finite human attention, since attention generates the engagement, data, and advertising revenue that fund "free" services.

**Panopticon**
: Bentham's prison design that Foucault made the master metaphor for modern power: a few watch the many, and the watched, unable to tell when they are observed, discipline themselves.

**Omnipticon**
: The surveillance architecture Serdar finds in social media: everyone watching everyone, with no central tower and no off-duty hours.

**Relational / generalized / institutional trust**
: Schilke, Reimann, and Cook's distinction between trust built between specific people, the default willingness to assume strangers are decent, and confidence in systems and rules.

**Content moderation**
: The rules and systems (policies, AI classifiers, human reviewers) by which platforms decide what content stays up, comes down, or gets demoted or labeled.

**Community notes**
: A crowd-based fact-checking system in which volunteer contributors write corrective notes on misleading posts, shown publicly only when contributors who usually disagree rate them helpful.
