## The Ladder Problem

Picture the first job a marketing graduate lands: drafting social posts, summarizing campaign results, cleaning up a deck for the senior strategist. Now reread that sentence as a list of prompts. The uncomfortable truth behind the last section's canary data is that the traditional entry-level job is substantially made of the tasks current AI does best: routine drafting, summarizing, first-pass analysis. Those tasks were never the point of the job, exactly. They were the *tuition*, how juniors absorbed the business while producing something useful enough to justify a salary.

That's the ladder problem. If AI takes over the bottom rungs, the question is not only "will there be jobs?" but "how does anyone climb to the jobs that remain?" Nobody has a complete answer yet. But the research does say useful things about which skills hold their value, and about the two kinds of adaptation everyone will keep telling you to do.

## Upskilling, Reskilling, and Why the Difference Matters

This week's Morandini reading brings some needed precision to a conversation usually conducted in buzzwords. **Upskilling** means deepening and extending the skills you already use, so you can keep doing your job as it transforms: the accountant learning to supervise AI-drafted analyses instead of building every spreadsheet by hand. **Reskilling** is the more radical move, retraining for substantially different work because your current role is disappearing or you're moving to a new one. Morandini and colleagues examine how organizations approach both, and the distinction matters because the two paths have completely different costs, timelines, and failure modes.

Upskilling is incremental and usually happens inside a job you already hold; the main risks are that training is superficial or that the job changes faster than the training does. Reskilling asks far more (time, money, identity), and it's where the hard questions about responsibility concentrate. Who pays for it? Employers have obvious incentives to invest in the workers they're keeping and quietly underinvest in the ones they're not. Morandini's organizational lens is useful here because it treats adaptation as something institutions do well or badly rather than a matter of individual grit. When you hear a company or a politician say "workers will simply need to reskill," the course's standing question applies: *simply* for whom, and at whose expense?

| | Upskilling | Reskilling |
|---|---|---|
| What changes | Depth and scope of your current skill set | Your occupation or core skill set |
| Typical trigger | Your job's tasks are being transformed | Your job's tasks are being eliminated or you're changing fields |
| Cost and disruption | Lower; often continuous, on the job | Higher: time out, income risk, identity shift |
| Main failure mode | Training that's too shallow or too slow | Nobody willing to fund it; retraining toward the wrong target |

## What Machines Make More Valuable

So which skills should the upskilling aim at? A useful filter: look for the parts of work where the machine's output is not the finished product, where something still has to pass through a human before it counts.

Four categories keep surfacing. **Judgment**: deciding what's worth doing, what "good" looks like, and when the plausible-sounding answer is wrong. That is the skill you've been building every time this course asked you to check an AI system's fluent output against reality. **Context**: knowing this client, this codebase, this community, this regulatory environment, all the local knowledge no general-purpose model was trained on. **Responsibility**: someone must be accountable for the decision, sign the audit, take the malpractice risk; organizations pay for accountability, and models cannot legally or meaningfully hold it. **Interpersonal work**: negotiating, caring, teaching, persuading, de-escalating, all tasks where a human on the other end is the entire point.

This is also the heart of David Autor's argument in this week's video, and it's worth watching him make it at full strength. Autor, an MIT labor economist and nobody's naive cheerleader, argues that AI's most important economic property may be its ability to *extend expertise*: to let workers with solid foundational skills, supported by AI, do valuable work that previously required elite credentials, the way earlier tools let nurse practitioners take on work once reserved for physicians. On this view the technology could help rebuild a hollowed-out middle class rather than finish it off. Notice what his argument requires, though: workers who can supply the judgment and responsibility around the machine's output. Expertise-extension is not a gift the technology gives; it's a deal, and your side of the deal is exactly the four categories above.

:::{seealso} When the Machine Teammate Fails
The optional Centeio Jorge reading studies human-agent teamwork and what happens to trust when the automation fails. The finding to carry with you: working *with* machines is a skill of calibration, meaning you trust the system the right amount, in the right situations, and know when to check. Teams that either blindly trust or reflexively distrust their automated teammate both underperform. "Managing AI work" is literal supervision, and supervising a tireless, confident, occasionally wrong subordinate is a genuinely new job skill.

## The History Lesson, Told Honestly

You will hear, correctly, that technology has always destroyed tasks and created others. The mechanization of agriculture eliminated most farm labor in industrialized countries, and the descendants of farmhands did not end up permanently unemployed. They ended up in factories, offices, hospitals, and jobs no nineteenth-century forecaster could have named. Whole occupations, telephone operator and typing pool among them, dissolved while employment grew. This is the strongest fact the optimists own, and your debate brief should not pretend otherwise.

But told honestly, the history lesson has a second half. The transitions were often brutal for the people who lived through them. "The economy created new jobs" is a statement about aggregates; it says nothing about whether the specific weaver, operator, or clerk who lost the old job got a new one at comparable pay, in the same town, within their working lifetime. The hard questions were never *whether* new work appears. They are *how fast* the old work disappears, whether the new work shows up in the same places for the same people, and who pays for the gap. The ILO's exposure gradients from last section sharpen the point: this transition arrives pre-sorted by gender and national income. History says adjustment happens. It does not say adjustment is painless, fast, or fair, and speed is the open variable this time.

## Advice You Can Actually Use

What does all this mean for someone entering the labor market in the next few years, into exactly the entry-level positions the canary data flags? Three strategies follow directly from the evidence.

**Build verification skills.** The one certainty about AI-saturated workplaces is that they will be full of fluent, confident, unverified output. The person who can rapidly tell sound from plausible (check the citation, test the code, catch the subtly wrong number) supplies the scarcest input in the room. Every fact-checking habit this course has drilled is labor-market preparation.

**Assemble a portfolio of judgment.** If routine output is cheap, proof that you produced routine output is worthless. What employers can no longer read off a résumé is judgment, so show it. Keep the projects where you can narrate the decisions: what you chose, what you rejected, what the AI drafted, and what you fixed and why. (This is precisely the skill your Futures Portfolio revision memo makes you practice: narrating your own process.)

**Learn to manage AI work.** Treat every tool you use this semester as management training. Delegate a task, specify quality, review the result, catch the failure, decide what you'd never delegate. Students who can only *do* tasks are competing with the model; students who can *direct and verify* work, the model's included, are doing the job one rung up.

None of this guarantees anything; the last section will show you serious people who read this same evidence and reach darker conclusions. But "the bottom rungs are exposed" is not a counsel of despair. It's a map of where not to stand still.
