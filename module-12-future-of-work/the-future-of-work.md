# Module 12: The Future of Work 💼

**Week 12 | Evidence for a Debate Everyone Is Having**

## Welcome

Everyone you know has an opinion about AI and jobs — your relatives, your group chat, the person interviewing you next spring. This week you trade opinions for evidence: what the best available data shows about AI's effect on employment so far, which skills the transition rewards, and how to argue about the future without pretending to know it. Your Futures Portfolio debate brief is due in draft form this week.

### 🎯 What You'll Learn

- What payroll data reveals about early-career workers in AI-exposed occupations — and why "canaries in the coal mine" is the researchers' own metaphor
- How generative-AI exposure is distributed worldwide, and why it lands differently by gender and country income
- What real usage data says about the split between automation (AI does the task instead of you) and augmentation (AI does it with you)
- The difference between upskilling and reskilling, and which human skills complement machines rather than compete with them
- How to build the strongest version of a position you may not hold — the skill your debate brief is graded on

### 🧠 Big Questions to Consider

- Can "entry-level effects are real" and "there is no mass unemployment" both be true at once?
- If the same AI system can automate a task or augment a worker, who decides which happens — and who lives with the choice?
- Technology has always destroyed some tasks and created others. Why is "it will work out eventually" not a complete answer?
- What should *you* do differently, starting now, if the bottom rungs of career ladders are the most exposed?
- When the evidence is genuinely mixed, what does intellectual honesty look like?

## This Week's Journey

We move in three steps. First, we lay four pieces of evidence side by side — payroll data, a global exposure map, real AI-usage logs, and the economy-wide null result from Module 4 — and practice holding their tensions honestly. Second, we get practical: skills, adaptation, and entering an AI-exposed labor market with your eyes open. Third, we turn evidence into argument: four positions, each at full strength, so you can pick a side for your brief and defend it like you mean it.

:::{note} Before Class
Read Brynjolfsson, Chandar, and Chen first — it's structured as six facts and anchors everything else. Skim the ILO update and the Anthropic Economic Index for their headline findings, and watch Autor's talk before Friday: he is the strongest version of the optimistic case, and your brief needs a strong opponent. Bring your draft debate brief to class — next week's workshop depends on it.
:::

| [The impact of AI on workers' skills: Upskilling and reskilling in organisations](https://www.informingscience.org/Publications/5078) | Morandini, S., et al. (2023). *Informing Science, 26*, 039–068. | Open access. The upskilling/reskilling distinction and what organizations do about it. |
| [How AI could help rebuild the middle class](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=66fYniAyCk0) | Autor, D. (2024). Video. | An MIT labor economist's optimistic case: AI could extend expertise to more workers. The steelman of augmentation optimism. |
