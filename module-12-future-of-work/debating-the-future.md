## This Is the Module Your Brief Comes From

Let's put the assignment on the table, because this section exists to serve it. Your Futures Portfolio, the capstone of this course, centers on a 1,200-word **debate brief**: the strongest case for one side of a future-of-work question, built on the Brynjolfsson, ILO, and Autor readings from this module. A complete draft is due this week. You'll bring it to next week's peer-review workshop, where two classmates read it against the same rubric your final will be graded with. Then you revise, and your finals-week submission includes a 300-word revision memo narrating what you changed and why.

Be clear about what the assignment is *not*. It is not a reflection on your feelings about AI, and it is not a prediction contest; you will not be graded in 2040 on whether you called it. It is an exercise in argument under uncertainty: taking a position while the evidence is genuinely mixed, using that evidence honestly, and confronting the strongest objection rather than the most convenient one. That skill has a name.

## Steelmanning: The Skill Being Graded

A **strawman** is the weak, distorted version of an opposing argument, easy to knock down, and the signature move of every bad take you've ever scrolled past. A **steelman** is the opposite: the *strongest* version of the opposing argument, stated so fairly that someone who holds it would accept your summary. Steelmanning is harder and rarer, and it is the difference between a brief that persuades and a brief that preaches.

Steelmanning works because your reader (your grader, and eventually your skeptical colleague or hiring manager) already knows the other side has real evidence. This module handed everyone the same four rulers. If your brief pretends the opposing evidence doesn't exist, the reader concludes you either didn't do the reading or hoped they didn't. Present the objection at full strength and *then* show why your position survives it, and you've borrowed the objection's credibility for your own conclusion. The concession paragraph is the load-bearing wall of a brief.

What follows are four positions, each steelmanned. One of them, or a sharper version you build yourself, is your brief.

## Four Positions at Full Strength

**Displacement pessimism: the canaries are the beginning.** The strong version of this position does not claim mass unemployment is here; it claims the leading indicators point down. Its best evidence is Brynjolfsson, Chandar, and Chen: a roughly 13 percent relative employment decline for early-career workers in the most AI-exposed occupations, in payroll data, already. Add the Anthropic usage logs showing that a substantial share of real-world use is automation-style, the task done instead of a person, and the pessimist asks a pointed question: why would firms that successfully automate junior tasks stop at junior tasks? Aggregate statistics always look fine until they don't; canaries exist because the air goes bad before the miners feel it. *Biggest weakness:* the same payroll data shows overall employment growing, the Yale Budget Lab's economy-wide indicators show no aggregate disruption, and every previous automation panic produced this exact argument, with early localized effects extrapolated to a collapse that never arrived. The pessimist must explain why this time the extrapolation holds when it never has before.

**Augmentation optimism: AI rebuilds the middle class.** This is Autor's position from this week's video, and its strong version is not "everything will be fine." It's a specific economic mechanism: expertise is scarce and expensive, and AI can extend it, allowing workers with good foundational skills to perform valuable work that previously required elite training, the way past tools expanded who could do skilled work. Its evidence: the Anthropic Index shows augmentation-style collaboration is a major share of actual use, the ILO expects transformation rather than elimination for most exposed occupations, and two centuries of history show technology creating tasks no forecaster imagined. *Biggest weakness:* the mechanism is conditional. It requires deployment choices that favor augmentation, institutions that train people for judgment, and a ladder for novices to become the experienced workers worth augmenting, while the canary data suggests the bottom of that ladder is exactly what's being sawed off. Optimism about the destination is not a plan for the transition.

**It's about power, not technology.** This position refuses the question "what will AI do to work?" as badly posed. Technologies don't do things to labor markets; employers, investors, and governments deploy technologies under rules those actors shape. Its best evidence is distributional: the ILO finds exposure skewed by gender and country income, so the technology lands on tilted ground, and the Anthropic Index shows adoption pooling unevenly across enterprises and geographies. Most tellingly, the *same capability* shows up in the usage data as both automation and augmentation, which means outcomes are being chosen, not dictated by the machine. Whether AI becomes Autor's expertise-extender or the pessimist's job-shredder is a question about bargaining power, management fashion, and policy, decided in deployment rather than in the model weights. *Biggest weakness:* pushed too far, it becomes unfalsifiable, since every outcome gets attributed to power after the fact, and it can understate genuine technical constraints. Some tasks really are newly automatable and some really aren't, and no negotiation changes that boundary; power determines a lot, but not everything.

**Too early to tell.** The least glamorous position, and often the hardest to defend well. Its strong version is not a shrug; it's a claim about evidence quality. Generative AI reached mass adoption only a few years ago; we are reading a handful of years of data about a transformation everyone concedes would take decades. The Yale Budget Lab finds nothing in the aggregate statistics. The canary finding, its authors' own question mark intact, could be AI displacement, or partly the residue of other forces cooling the entry-level market. Confident forecasts in both directions have embarrassed their authors before. *Biggest weakness:* "wait for better data" is itself a policy, one that guarantees any response begins late. The canaries are precisely the early-warning data this position discounts, and the people on the exposed rungs of the ladder can't postpone their career decisions until the time series matures.

| Position | Best evidence | Biggest weakness |
|---|---|---|
| Displacement pessimism | ~13% relative entry-level decline in exposed occupations (Brynjolfsson et al.); automation-style usage at scale (Anthropic) | Aggregates still growing (payroll data; Yale); every past panic extrapolated the same way |
| Augmentation optimism (Autor) | Augmentation share of real usage (Anthropic); ILO's transformation-over-elimination; history of task creation | Conditional on deployment choices; the entry-level ladder problem |
| Power and distribution | ILO's gender and income gradients; uneven adoption; same tech used both ways (Anthropic) | Risks unfalsifiability; understates real technical limits |
| Too early to tell | Yale's economy-wide null; short time series; forecasting's track record | Waiting is a choice with costs; discounts exactly the early-warning data we have |

:::{tip} Building the Brief
Pick the position you can argue best, not necessarily the one you privately hold; arguing a position you're unsure of is the assignment working as intended. Then: (1) narrow the question, since "will AI destroy work?" is unarguable in 1,200 words while "will AI hollow out entry-level knowledge work in the next decade?" is winnable; (2) lead with your best evidence, cited specifically, naming the study and the finding rather than "studies show"; (3) spend a full paragraph on your position's biggest weakness from the table above, at full strength, and answer it; (4) use only numbers you can trace to a source, because this module gave you real ones and inventing more is both unnecessary and the fastest way to lose a debate; and (5) include your one-sentence AI-use disclosure, as on every assignment. Bring the complete draft to class: your peer reviewers next week will be checking your steelman first.
:::
