# Module 14: Social Media and Society 📱

**Week 14 | The Feed That Watches Back**

## Welcome

You will spend time on social media this week. That is not a guess; it is a business plan. In our final taught module, we turn the course's toolkit on the platforms where most of the information society actually happens: who pays for the feed, what it learns about you while you scroll, and who decides what stays up. Every earlier thread (trust, misinformation, ethics, AI) comes together here.

### 🎯 What You'll Learn

- How Zuboff's theory of surveillance capitalism explains why the most powerful communication systems in history are free to use
- What "behavioral surplus" and "prediction products" are, and how they turn your attention into revenue
- How surveillance has shifted from the panopticon (a few watching many) to the omnipticon (everyone watching everyone), and what that does to trust
- What the evidence actually says about community-based fact-checking, and how to read a corporate policy announcement critically against that evidence
- How to argue, with reasons, about who should govern speech on platforms this central to public life

### 🧠 Big Questions to Consider

- If you're not paying for the product, what exactly is being sold, and is "you are the product" even the right slogan?
- What happens to friendship, honesty, and risk-taking when social life is permanently scored, recorded, and searchable?
- Who should decide what's true on platforms billions of people use: companies, crowds, governments, or no one?
- When a platform says a policy change is about "free expression," which definition of it are they using, and who benefits?
- After fourteen weeks: does technology shape society, does society shape technology, or are you inside a loop that runs both ways?

## This Week's Plan

We begin with the business model, because on social media the business model is the message: Zuboff's account of surveillance capitalism explains the machinery beneath every feed, and fresh Pew data on teens shows what living inside it feels like. Then we turn the camera around, from platforms watching users to users watching each other, with a *Black Mirror* episode and the sociology of trust. We close with the most consequential live experiment in platform governance: the 2025 shift from professional fact-checking to community notes, and the question of who should referee reality.

:::{note} Before Class
Read Zuboff's "Big Other" first. It is the week's anchor and its densest text, so give it real time. Then read Kaplan's Meta announcement and the Slaughter et al. study back to back, noting where the announcement's claims and the study's evidence touch, and where they don't. Skim the Pew report and bring one finding that matched your experience and one that didn't.
:::
