## A Five-Star Life

The *Black Mirror* episode "Nosedive" opens on a woman named Lacie practicing her laugh in the mirror. In her world, every human interaction ends with a rating: one to five stars, delivered by phone, instantly averaged into a public score that follows you everywhere. The score is infrastructural. It determines what apartments you can rent, what flights you can book, which events you're welcome at. So Lacie curates. She photographs artfully bitten cookies she doesn't want to eat. She rehearses spontaneity. She is, every waking moment, performing for a jury of everyone.

The episode is fiction, and pointedly exaggerated. But this week's Serdar reading treats it as something more useful than prophecy: a laboratory. By pushing peer rating to its logical extreme, "Nosedive" isolates a real dynamic that already runs through ordinary social media, and to see it clearly, Serdar argues, you need a concept with a two-hundred-year pedigree.

## From the Watchtower to the Feed

In the late eighteenth century, the philosopher Jeremy Bentham proposed a prison design he called the **panopticon**: cells arranged in a ring around a central watchtower, backlit so every prisoner is visible from the center, while the tower's interior stays dark. The genius of the design, or the horror of it, is that the guard doesn't need to watch everyone at once. Because prisoners *can't tell* when they're being observed, they must act as if they always are. Surveillance gets internalized; the watched become their own guards.

Two centuries later, Michel Foucault made the panopticon the master metaphor of modern power. Discipline, he argued, no longer needs chains when it has visibility: schools, factories, hospitals, and bureaucracies all quietly teach us to behave as though observed, until the observer can go home and the behavior remains. Note the architecture of this power: it is asymmetrical. The few watch the many, and the many cannot watch back. When people call the data-harvesting platforms of the last section a "digital panopticon," this is the lineage they're invoking. Zuboff's Big Other is a watchtower whose interior you cannot inspect.

But Serdar's central claim is that the panopticon no longer describes what social media *feels* like from inside, because the architecture has changed. On Instagram or TikTok, the watcher is not a guard in a tower. It is everyone. Your friends, your followers, strangers, future employers, that guy from high school, all potentially in the audience, all equipped to record, screenshot, and judge. Serdar calls this the **omnipticon**: everyone watching everyone, surveillance with no center, no tower, and no off-duty hours. You are simultaneously prisoner and guard, performer and jury, like Lacie rating others between takes of her own performance.

The shift matters because it changes who enforces the discipline. In the panopticon, you internalize the gaze of an institution. In the omnipticon, you internalize the gaze of your *peers*, and peers are everywhere, inconsistent, and impossible to appeal to. Hence the distinctive labor of contemporary online life, which "Nosedive" renders literal: the drafted-and-deleted post, the agonized selection among nearly identical photos, the maintenance of a self designed for review. Sociologists have long known that everyone performs different selves for different audiences; what's new is that social media merges those audiences into one permanent, recording crowd, and attaches metrics to the performance. Likes, followers, ratios, streaks: reputation, quantified.

:::{seealso} The Watchtower Didn't Go Away
Don't let the omnipticon replace the panopticon in your analysis; stack them. While everyone watches everyone, the platform watches *all of it*, harvesting the whole theater as behavioral surplus. Peer surveillance generates the engagement; corporate surveillance monetizes it. The two architectures are not rivals but roommates, and any full account of social media needs both.

## What Scoring Does to Trust

Here is where the week's sociology reading earns its place. Schilke, Reimann, and Cook's review of trust research distinguishes forms of trust that operate at different ranges. **Relational trust** is particular: built between specific people through repeated interaction, vulnerability, and history. You trust your best friend because of a thousand small proofs. **Generalized trust** is diffuse: your default willingness to assume strangers are probably decent, the background confidence that lets societies of strangers function at all. And **institutional trust** is trust in systems (courts, banks, platforms) to make interactions safe even when you know nothing about the person on the other side.

Now run the omnipticon through that framework. Relational trust is built precisely through unguarded interaction: being seen unedited, and finding out it's safe. But if social life is performed for a recording audience, unguardedness gets scarce. What Serdar's omnipticon threatens is the raw material of intimacy. It is hard to be *known* while you are being *reviewed*. "Nosedive" dramatizes the end state, where every relationship in the episode, including family, is strategic, because candor is priced in stars.

Generalized trust takes a different hit. Reputation scores look, at first, like a trust *technology*: don't trust the stranger, trust their rating. But look closely at the substitution. Schilke and colleagues' framework helps you see that scores don't so much build generalized trust as *replace* it with institutional trust, trust in the scoring system itself. And that system now inherits every question you learned to ask this semester. Who designed the metric? Can it be gamed, bought, or brigaded? What does it actually measure, trustworthiness or skill at performing it? A society that outsources trust to scores is betting everything on the integrity of the scorer.

## Real-World Echoes, Handled with Care

Once you have the omnipticon and the trust framework, you start seeing partial versions of "Nosedive" everywhere, but *partial* is the operative word, and precision matters more than drama.

The ratings economies are the closest fit: ride-share drivers and passengers rate each other, hosts and guests review each other, delivery workers and freelancers live and die by their stars. For customers this is a minor courtesy ritual; for workers it is the omnipticon as employment condition, where a bad week of ratings can mean deactivation by algorithm. Note the asymmetry the "everyone watches everyone" framing can hide: mutual rating does not mean mutual stakes.

Cancel dynamics, the sudden networked pile-on, are the omnipticon's enforcement arm. The behavior itself, communities sanctioning norm violations, is ancient. What's new is scale, speed, permanence, and audience collapse: a remark made for one context is judged by every context at once, and the record never expires. Whether any given pile-on is accountability or cruelty is usually the wrong first question; the structural point is that the *capacity* for instant mass judgment now sits under every post.

Finally, the social credit debates: proposals and pilot systems, most prominently discussed in relation to China, for scoring citizens' conduct and attaching consequences. Reporting on these systems has often outrun their reality, since coverage has at times described something far more unified and dystopian than what verifiably exists, so treat specific claims skeptically. But the *debate* is instructive on its own: it forces the question of what happens when reputation scoring is run by a state rather than a platform, with exit no longer an option. That question doesn't need a foreign villain, though. It should also make you look again at the scoring systems you already live inside voluntarily, and at how voluntary they really are when the unscored life carries its own price.
