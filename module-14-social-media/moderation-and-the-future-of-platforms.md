## The Job Nobody Can Do and Nobody Can Skip

Every platform moderates. The most committed "free speech" platform still removes spam, scams, and illegal content. Without that floor, a feed becomes unusable in weeks. So **content moderation** (the rules and systems deciding what stays up, what comes down, and what gets demoted or labeled) is not optional; it is constitutive. The choice is never *whether* to edit the feed, only how, by whom, and how visibly.

The scale is the first thing to grasp: billions of posts per day, in every language, arriving faster than any human workforce could read. Modern moderation is therefore mostly AI. Machine learning classifiers make the first pass, human reviewers handle appeals and hard calls, and written policies try to compress the world's moral disagreements into enforceable rules. Every layer fails in its own way. Classifiers misread sarcasm, satire, dialect, and context; human review at scale is grueling work; and the policies must settle contested questions (what counts as hate, as medical misinformation, as incitement) on which democracies themselves don't agree. Every error lands as either censorship, meaning true speech removed, or negligence, meaning harmful content amplified, and reducing one error type generally increases the other. Hold that trade-off in mind; it is the honest core of everything that follows.

## Reading the Announcement Like an Analyst

On January 7, 2025, Meta's chief global affairs officer Joel Kaplan published "More Speech and Fewer Mistakes," announcing that Meta would end its third-party fact-checking program in the United States and replace it with a community notes system, while loosening some content policies and scaling back proactive enforcement to focus on the most severe violations. Whatever you think of it, it is one of the most consequential platform-governance documents of the decade, which is why you're reading the primary source rather than coverage of it.

You've spent a semester learning to read documents like this, so apply the training. Start with what the announcement *claims*: that fact-checkers, like all experts, have biases; that too much legitimate speech was being caught in enforcement's nets; that a community-driven system is less prone to institutional bias; that the change means more speech and fewer mistakes. Some of these claims contain real substance. Over-enforcement is a genuine and well-documented problem, and the previous section's trade-off guarantees that any enforcement regime produces false positives that feel, to the people silenced, exactly like censorship.

Now look at what the framing *does*. The title decides the conclusion before the argument starts: who is against more speech and fewer mistakes? "Bias" is asserted about fact-checkers largely by pointing to perceptions of bias, which do exist, rather than to systematic error rates. The mistakes counted are removals of legitimate speech; mistakes in the other direction, false content left to spread, quietly exit the ledger. And the document's timing invites the question it never addresses: announced two weeks before a new U.S. administration took office, following years of political pressure over moderation decisions. None of this tells you the policy is wrong. It tells you the announcement is an argument by an interested party, a company whose flywheel (as you saw in the first section) runs on engagement, and arguments by interested parties get checked against independent evidence.

## What the Evidence Actually Shows

Conveniently, independent evidence exists, and it cuts in an interesting direction: partly *for* the crowd, on the platform that pioneered the approach. The Slaughter et al. study in *PNAS* analyzed X's Community Notes, the system in which volunteer contributors write corrective notes on misleading posts, where a note becomes publicly visible only when contributors who normally *disagree* with each other rate it helpful. The study found that false posts receiving a visible community note drew measurably less engagement and spread less widely than they otherwise would have. That is a genuinely important result: crowd-checking is not just a slogan, and the wisdom-of-crowds mechanism can demonstrably dampen the diffusion of specific false claims.

But read the finding at its actual size, the way you learned to in Module 8. It shows that notes work *on the posts that get them, once they appear*. It does not show that most false content ever receives a note, that notes arrive before the crucial early hours when viral posts do most of their traveling, or that the bridging mechanism holds up on the most polarizing claims, the ones where getting opposing camps to agree is hardest precisely because the stakes are highest. Whether a community notes system can carry the *entire* weight previously shared with professional fact-checkers, across languages, topics, and coordinated manipulation campaigns, is exactly the experiment the industry launched in 2025, on live populations, with results still coming in. The intellectually honest position holds both halves: Kaplan's announcement leans on a mechanism with real evidence behind it, and extends that mechanism far beyond what the evidence has tested.

:::{warning} Beware the Two Lazy Endings
Resist the two conclusions that require no thought. "Community notes are a cynical excuse to stop moderating" ignores real evidence that crowd-checking reduces engagement with noted falsehoods, and the real costs of the expert-gatekeeper model it replaced. "The crowd has solved misinformation" ignores that coverage, speed, and adversarial robustness remain open questions. The evidence supports something less quotable: crowds demonstrably help where they act, and no one yet knows if they're enough. Analysts live in that middle; partisans vacation at the ends.
:::

## Who Should Decide What's True?

Zoom out and the 2025 pivot is one move in a much older game: locating the referee. Infrastructure this central to public life has exactly four candidate governors, and by now you have the tools to weigh them.

| Referee | Strongest case for | Strongest case against |
|---|---|---|
| Companies | They own the systems, act fast, see the data | Unelected, profit-driven (remember the flywheel), globally inconsistent |
| Crowds | Legitimacy, scale, no single point of capture, plus Slaughter-style evidence they can work | Coverage gaps, speed, vulnerable to brigading; majorities can be wrong |
| States | Democratic mandate, real enforcement power | Governments are frequent *sources* of misinformation; "truth ministries" age badly |
| No one | No censor to capture; maximal speech | The last section's flywheel keeps amplifying whatever engages; abdication is also a choice |

Every row is a real argument; this is not a table with a secret right answer. The world is also running the experiment in parallel: the European Union's regulatory approach obliges large platforms to assess and mitigate systemic risks, the United States in 2025–26 leans toward platform self-governance and crowd mechanisms, and other governments claim direct control. Your generation will get the comparative data. The institutional-trust lens from Schilke, Reimann, and Cook is the right one to bring: every referee option is really a proposal about *which institution deserves the trust*, and trust, once spent, is the one resource none of them can quickly rebuild.

## You're in the Loop

End where the course began. In Module 1 you met the big framing debate: does technology drive society, or does society steer technology? Fourteen weeks later you can see why the question was a trap. Surveillance capitalism was not decreed by the microchip. It was a business-model choice, made by particular companies at particular moments, that law, competition, and users are now contesting. The omnipticon emerged from platform design, but norms about screenshots, receipts, and grace are being negotiated in every group chat. The moderation pivot was corporate strategy shaped by political pressure, now being tested by researchers, regulators, and, post by post, the crowds writing the notes. Technology shapes society; society shapes it right back, through law, norms, markets, and use. And there is no outside position from which to merely observe: you rate, you post, you scroll, you vote; someday you may design, regulate, or report. The loop runs through you.

That is not a burden. It's the point of the course. Systems built by choices can be rebuilt by choices, and you now know where the choice points are.
