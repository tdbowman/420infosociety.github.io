# Module 10: Information Communication and Society 📡

**Week 10 | How Technology Comes Between Us — and What It Does There**

## Welcome

Almost nothing you know about the world arrived unmediated: it came through a screen, a feed, a map, a message, or — increasingly — a machine that talks back. This week we stop looking *at* our communication technologies and start asking what they *do* while they sit between us and everything else. Once you can see mediation, you can never quite unsee it.

### 🎯 What You'll Learn

- What it means to say technologies "mediate" experience — and why the old "tools are neutral" story doesn't survive contact with your own phone
- Don Ihde's four human–technology relations (embodiment, hermeneutic, alterity, background) and how to spot each one in an ordinary day
- How Verbeek extends this picture: technologies help shape both the world you perceive and the person you become
- What the Reuters Institute's 2026 data reveals about a news ecosystem now led by social and video platforms — and increasingly narrated by AI
- How to apply "mediation literacy": a practical set of questions to ask of any technology that carries your communication

### 🧠 Big Questions to Consider

- If a technology changes what you notice, remember, and say, in what sense is it "just a tool"?
- When an editor, an algorithm, and a chatbot each hand you "the news," what did each one quietly decide for you?
- Why does trust in news keep falling even as access to news keeps expanding?
- When a teenager confides in an AI chatbot, is that communication *through* a machine or *with* one — and does the difference matter?
- Who benefits when we stop noticing our mediations?

## This Week's Journey

We move from theory to ecosystem to relationship. First, the conceptual toolkit: postphenomenology, Ihde's four relations, Verbeek's mediation theory, and multistability. Second, we point that toolkit at the news — from edited front pages to algorithmic feeds to AI summaries — using the Reuters Institute's freshest global data. Third, we follow the strangest development of all: machines that have stopped being channels and become conversation partners, especially for the youngest users among us.
