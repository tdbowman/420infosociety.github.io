# Communicating with Machines

## The Quasi-Other Goes Mainstream

For most of the history of communication technology, the machine was the *middle* of the conversation, never the other end of it. The telephone carried a voice; the voice belonged to a person. Even the early internet, for all its strangeness, was overwhelmingly people talking to people through machines.

That era is ending, quietly and quickly, and the clearest evidence comes from the youngest users. This week's Pew Research Center reading, *Teens, Social Media and AI Chatbots 2025*, reports that about 64% of U.S. teens have used AI chatbots, and roughly three in ten use them daily. Sit with the second number. Daily use is not experimentation; it is habit. For a substantial fraction of American teenagers, conversing with software is now as ordinary as texting, part of the routine texture of a day. Whatever your own habits, you are living through the mainstreaming of something philosophy saw coming decades ago: Ihde's **alterity relation**, in which a technology stops being a window or a display and becomes a *quasi-other*, something we face, address, and respond to almost as we would a person.

When Ihde described alterity relations, his examples were charmingly modest: the ATM that "asks" for your PIN, the toy robot, the automated phone menu. Nobody confided in an ATM. The relation's structure hasn't changed, but its depth has. A modern chatbot holds context, mirrors your tone, remembers what you told it, and produces responses tuned, by training processes we examined earlier in the course, to be helpful, agreeable, and warm. The quasi-other has become good company. That's an astonishing technical achievement and a genuinely new situation for human communication, and it deserves better than either panic or cheerleading.

## Channel or Partner?

Start with a distinction the last section set up. When you text a friend, the technology is a **channel**: it mediates between two humans, and the interesting questions are about what the channel amplifies and reduces (tone gets lost; response time becomes meaningful; the read receipt turns silence into a message). When you talk with a chatbot, the technology is the **partner**: there is no human on the other end, and the interesting questions change entirely.

That shift strains the concepts you have been building all semester. Communication, in every model this course has touched, involved meaning on both ends: someone intending, someone interpreting. A chatbot produces fluent, contextually apt responses without anything we can confidently call intending. So when a teenager spends an hour in conversation with one, what exactly happened? One honest answer is that a real experience of being heard was produced by a system that did no hearing. Both halves of that sentence are true, and the tension between them is the defining puzzle of this module. Verbeek's framework helps here more than alarm does. Set aside whether the chatbot "really" understands and ask what this mediation *does* to the person in it: what it amplifies, what it invites, and what kind of self it helps constitute over thousands of conversations.

## What's Gained

Be fair to the gains first; they are substantial. **Access:** a chatbot never sleeps, never judges (or never appears to), costs little or nothing, and waits patiently through questions a student might be too embarrassed to ask a teacher about a concept, a diagnosis, a feeling. For people isolated by geography, disability, language, or social anxiety, an always-available interlocutor is not a toy. It can be the difference between asking and not asking.

**Practice:** conversation is a skill, and machines offer a rehearsal space with the stakes removed. Drafting a hard apology, practicing an interview, trying out an argument before bringing it to a real person: these are old writing-as-thinking strategies with a responsive partner added.

**Availability itself:** at 3 a.m., when the humans who love you are asleep, something that responds kindly is not nothing. Pretending otherwise would be dishonest, and cruel to the people for whom this matters.

## What's at Stake

Now the other column, and here the callbacks accumulate. First, **formulaic intimacy**. A chatbot's warmth is a product feature: generated, optimized, and identical in kind to the warmth it extends to millions of others simultaneously. Human intimacy is costly. It requires time, risk, and the real possibility of being refused, and that costliness is part of what it *means*. A relationship with an entity that cannot leave, cannot be bored, and cannot be hurt offers the sensations of intimacy without its structure. The feeling isn't fake; the feeling is real. The risk is that frictionless practice becomes the standard, and the friction of human others starts to read as malfunction.

Second, **data**. Everything said to a chatbot is said to a company. The disclosures people make to a quasi-other, precisely because it feels private, judgment-free, and consequence-free, can be more revealing than anything they post publicly. Recall the questions about data, platform power, and surveillance you worked through in Modules 6 and 7, then consider that a confiding conversation is the richest behavioral data yet invented.

Third, **dependency**. The same availability that makes chatbots valuable at 3 a.m. makes them easy to reach for at every other hour too: before a friend, before a parent, before sitting alone with a thought. Pew's daily-use figure is not proof of dependency, but it marks the ground on which dependency can grow for some users, a dynamic familiar from the persuasive-design patterns of social platforms you studied earlier in the course.

## Mediation Literacy: Five Questions

This module ends the way the course keeps ending, with a practice rather than a verdict. You cannot opt out of mediation, but you can interrogate it. Here are five questions to ask of *any* communication technology (a feed, a chatbot, a group chat, whatever arrives next), distilled from everything this module has built.

1. **Which relation am I in?** Am I perceiving through this technology, reading the world off of it, addressing it as a quasi-other, or being shaped by it from the background? Naming the relation is half the analysis.
2. **What does this mediation amplify, and what does it reduce?** Every channel and every partner makes some things loud and others inaudible. What is missing from what I'm getting?
3. **What is it optimizing for?** My understanding, my attention, my retention, my data? Whose goals does the tilt of this technology serve, and would its designers state those goals out loud?
4. **What is it inviting me to do, and to become?** Verbeek's deepest point is that mediations shape selves. What habits, expectations, and capacities does regular use of this technology cultivate in me? Do I endorse that person?
5. **What would I lose if it vanished tomorrow?** The answer measures dependency, and often reveals, more honestly than introspection, what the technology has actually been doing there.

These questions are the module's real deliverable. The theories will blur; the habit of asking can last.
