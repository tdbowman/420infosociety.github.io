# How Technology Mediates Experience

## Your Phone Is Editing Your Memory

Sometime this morning, your phone probably showed you a memory. Its memory, not yours: a photo from this day three years ago, maybe set to music, maybe grouped with others its software decided belonged together. You smiled, felt a pulse of nostalgia, and moved on. But pause on what just happened. A device selected one moment from the thousands you've lived, decided it was worth remembering, and delivered it back with an emotional soundtrack. Over years, that selection shapes which parts of your own life feel vivid and which fade. Nobody voted on this. It just became how remembering works.

Or consider how you know your city. If you navigate by Maps, you know your city as a series of routes: efficient corridors between destinations, with everything off-route dimmed into irrelevance. Someone who learned the same city by wandering knows a different city, one with texture between the destinations. Neither of you knows "the city itself." You each know the city *as mediated* by a particular way of moving through it.

This is the starting insight of the tradition we take up this week, called **postphenomenology**. It's a mouthful of a word for a simple, radical idea. You cannot understand technology by studying devices, or human experience by studying people, because in a technological society the two are entangled all the way down. What we should study is the *relation*: the specific way a technology sits between a person and their world, and what it does while it sits there. This week's Verbeek readings call that in-between work **mediation**, and their central claim is worth writing on your wall. Technologies are not neutral instruments waiting for good or bad uses. They actively shape what shows up in our world, how it shows up, and what we do about it, all before any question of "use" arises.

## Ihde's Four Relations

The philosopher Don Ihde, whose framework Verbeek builds on, noticed that technologies don't all sit between us and the world in the same way. He mapped four distinct relations, each with its own little schema. Learn these four and you will start seeing them everywhere.

:name: fig-ihde-four-relations
:alt: A diagram with four labeled rows, one per human–technology relation. The embodiment row shows a human and glasses joined in parentheses with an arrow pointing to the world; the hermeneutic row shows a human with an arrow pointing to a thermometer and world joined in parentheses; the alterity row shows a human with an arrow pointing to a chatbot while a faded, dashed world box sits in parentheses behind it; the background row shows a human connected by a dashed line to a thermostat and world joined in parentheses, and each row ends with a plain-language example sentence.
:width: 100%

Ihde's four human–technology relations. The parentheses mark what fuses together in each relation; the arrow marks where your attention points.
:::

In the **embodiment relation** (schematically, *(human–technology) → world*) the technology fuses with your body and becomes the thing you perceive *through*. Glasses are the classic case: you don't look at your glasses, you look through them at the world, and when they're working well you forget they exist. Your phone approaches this when texting becomes so fluent that the keyboard disappears from awareness and you're simply *talking* to someone.

In the **hermeneutic relation** (*human → (technology–world)*) you perceive the technology itself and *read* the world off of it. A thermometer doesn't let you feel the temperature; it gives you a number that you interpret as the temperature. ("Hermeneutic" just means interpretive.) Your weather app, your fitness tracker's sleep score, a news headline: in each case the world reaches you as a representation that had to be constructed, by someone or something, according to choices you usually can't see.

In the **alterity relation** (*human → technology (world)*) the technology stops being a window or a display and becomes a *thing you interact with*, a "quasi-other," while the world recedes into the background. Ihde had ATMs and toys in mind; we have something far stranger. When you ask a chatbot to explain a concept and it answers, apologizes, and asks a clarifying question, you are in an alterity relation with software. Hold that thought. The third section of this module is entirely about it.

In the **background relation** (*human (technology/world)*) the technology shapes your experience without ever being an object of it. The thermostat conditions the room. The refrigerator hums. Your phone's notification settings silently structure which parts of the world are allowed to interrupt you. Background technologies may be the most powerful of all because they never ask for attention.

:::{tip} Try This Today
Pick one ordinary hour and catch each relation in the act: something you perceived *through* (embodiment), something you *read the world off of* (hermeneutic), something you *interacted with as a quasi-other* (alterity), and something that shaped the hour *without your noticing* (background). Most students find the fourth is the hardest, which is exactly the point.
:::

One warning as you use the framework: these are relations, not categories of device. The same phone is embodied while you type, hermeneutic while you check the weather, an alterity while you argue with its assistant, and background while it decides, face-down on the table, which buzzes get through.

## Verbeek's Extension: Technologies Make Worlds and Selves

Ihde's map describes where technology sits. Verbeek pushes further and asks what mediation *does*, and his answer has two parts.

First, technologies mediate **perception** by amplifying some aspects of the world and reducing others. Verbeek's signature example is obstetric ultrasound. It makes the fetus visible as a separate little patient, measurable and diagnosable, weeks before birth, and in doing so it hands expectant parents a set of decisions that simply did not exist before the technology. What the ultrasound amplifies (the fetus as individual) and what it reduces (the pregnancy as a unified bodily experience) changes what there *is* to think about.

Second, technologies mediate **action** by inviting some behaviors and inhibiting others. A speed bump doesn't argue with you about safe driving; it simply makes slow driving the path of least resistance. A "read receipt" makes silence legible, and therefore makes ignoring a message a *statement* in a way it never was with paper mail. No one is forced in any of these cases. Mediation rarely coerces. It tilts.

Put the two together and you get Verbeek's most striking claim: technologies help constitute not only the world we experience but the **selves** we become. The you who navigates by Maps, remembers by camera roll, and stays reachable by default is not a pre-technological self who happens to use tools. Those mediations are partly *how* you perceive, decide, remember, and relate. This is a description rather than a lament, and it applies as much to writing, eyeglasses, and clocks as to anything with a battery. The question was never whether to be technologically mediated (that ship sailed millennia ago) but *which* mediations we build, accept, and hand on.

## Multistability: One Technology, Many Technologies

There is one more idea in the toolkit, and it saves the whole framework from determinism: **multistability**. A technology, Ihde argued, never has just one stable meaning or use; it settles into different "stabilities" in different hands, cultures, and contexts. The same smartphone is a lifeline for a rural patient reaching a distant doctor, a leash for an employee whose boss expects replies at midnight, a stage for a teenager building an audience, and a slot machine for a commuter refreshing a feed. None of these is the phone's "true" use. Each is a genuine stability: a durable way the same artifact and different human intentions lock together.

Multistability matters because it blocks two lazy conclusions at once. Against the enthusiasts: no, the technology's benefits are not guaranteed by its design, because design doesn't determine use. Against the doomers: no, the harms aren't inevitable either, for the same reason. What multistability leaves us with is responsibility. If technologies tilt but don't force, and if the same artifact can stabilize in better and worse ways, then the tilt is something we can study, criticize, and redesign. That's the course's recurring question in new clothes: which mediation is at work here, and who benefits from it?
