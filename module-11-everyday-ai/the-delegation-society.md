# The Delegation Society

## From Using to Delegating

Picture a Tuesday morning in the near future (or, depending on your settings, this Tuesday morning). Overnight, your calendar agent noticed a conflict and rescheduled a meeting, choosing which of two people to inconvenience. Your shopping agent reordered groceries, substituting a brand because your usual one cost more this week. Your email agent answered three routine messages in your voice and flagged one it thought you should see. You did none of this. You woke up to a report.

None of these actions is dramatic, and that is exactly the point. The technology story of the 2020s is a thousand small transfers of agency rather than one spectacular robot: moments where software stopped waiting for your hand and started acting on your behalf. This module calls the result the *delegation society*, a world where the default relationship between people and computers is shifting from use to delegation.

The shift is easiest to see as a spectrum. At one end sits the **tool**: a calculator, a spellchecker. You do the task; the tool amplifies you. Next comes the **assistant**: autocomplete drafts your sentence, but you approve every word. Then the **adviser**: the maps app picks a route, the feed decides what you see next. You still technically choose, but from a menu something else assembled. At the far end sits the **delegate**, or in the current jargon, the *agent*: software that takes a goal, performs a multi-step task in the world (booking, paying, emailing), and reports back afterward. If you watched Sivasubramanian's TED talk from the optional list, you heard this end of the spectrum described with real excitement. It is worth noticing that the people most excited about agents are often the people selling them.

Two caveats about the spectrum. First, the same product can sit at several points at once: a chatbot is an assistant when it drafts, an adviser when it recommends, an agent when you let it send. Second, movement along the spectrum is rarely a decision you make. It arrives as a default setting, a new feature, a checkbox pre-checked on your behalf.

## Executor, Adviser, Interlocutor

This week's anchor reading, the Ada Lovelace Institute briefing by Farmer and Smakman, gives us a sharper vocabulary for the right-hand side of the spectrum. Advanced AI assistants, they argue, play three overlapping roles in everyday life. As **executors**, they carry out tasks: scheduling, purchasing, filing, filtering. As **advisers**, they shape decisions: what to buy, whom to reply to, how to phrase the difficult text. And as **interlocutors**, they talk *with* us: companions, tutors, sounding boards, entities we address in the second person and sometimes, uncomfortably, confide in.

The triad matters because each role carries a different risk profile. An executor that fails costs you a booking. An adviser that fails, or that quietly optimizes for someone else's interests, bends thousands of your small choices without leaving fingerprints. An interlocutor that fails can distort something more intimate: your sense of being understood. Farmer and Smakman's central warning is that all three roles are converging into single products, sold by a small number of firms, woven into operating systems, and adopted through convenience rather than deliberation.

:::{note} A very old arrangement, minus the safeguards
Delegation itself is ancient. Rich and busy people have always had agents: travel agents, stockbrokers, lawyers, personal secretaries. But human agents come wrapped in centuries of accumulated safeguards. Professional licenses. Fiduciary duties (a legal obligation to act in *your* interest, not their own). Malpractice liability. And the simple fact that a person can be fired, sued, or shamed. AI agents arrive with the delegation but, so far, without most of the wrapper. The question this module keeps asking is not "should delegation exist?" but "where did the safeguards go?"
:::

## What Changes When Software Acts for You

Start with the psychology. Delegation outsources attention as surely as it outsources labor. When your agent handles the calendar, you stop rehearsing the week in your head. When it answers routine email, you stop practicing the small diplomacy of declining politely. Researchers call the long-run effect **skill atrophy**: capacities that fade because they are no longer exercised. You already know the mild version. How many phone numbers do you know by heart? The delegation society scales that experience from memory to judgment: navigation, budgeting, scheduling, phrasing, even choosing gifts.

Atrophy shades into **dependency**, one of the core risks in the Farmer and Smakman briefing. Dependency means more than heavy use. It is the state in which *not* using a service becomes costly or frightening, when you can no longer comfortably do the task or no longer trust yourself to. Dependency also changes your bargaining position: a service you cannot leave is a service that can change its terms.

Then there is the social layer. When your agent emails my agent to find a meeting time, who exactly is having a relationship? Small acts of consideration (remembering a birthday, writing the condolence note yourself) carry meaning *because* they cost effort. Delegating them saves the effort and, in the same motion, drains some of the meaning. The Pew survey you will read for Section 2 finds Americans especially uneasy about AI entering relationships, and this is plausibly why: some tasks are not chores attached to a relationship, they are the relationship itself.

## The Accountability Question

Now the hard part. Suppose your shopping agent, optimizing for price, buys from a seller who never ships. Suppose your email agent, writing in your voice, offends a colleague. Suppose a scheduling agent quietly deprioritizes the same person every week. Who is accountable?

On the left of the spectrum the answer is easy: you used a tool, the results are yours. On the right, responsibility smears across at least four parties: you (who set the goal loosely), the developer (who trained the system), the platform (whose defaults shaped its behavior), and the counterparties (who may have been dealing with an agent without knowing it). Everyone contributed; no one decided. Philosophers of technology call this the *problem of many hands*, and Farmer and Smakman flag unclear accountability as one of the defining governance gaps of the assistant era.

:::{warning} "The AI did it" is not an answer
Watch for accountability laundering: using the agent's autonomy as a shield. "The algorithm made the decision" has already appeared in contexts from loan denials to content moderation. A useful rule from this course's running thread: autonomy talk that would sound absurd about a human employee ("my assistant sent it, so it's not my fault") should sound equally absurd about a software one, unless someone can show you where the responsibility actually went.
:::

## Whose Agents Are They, Anyway?

Finally, follow the power. An agent is only as loyal as its incentives, and you are not the only party writing them. If your agent is built, hosted, and updated by one of three or four companies (the same companies that run the app stores, the ad markets, and the cloud), then "your" delegate is also a channel for someone else's interests. Which brand does the shopping agent substitute? Whose travel inventory does it search first? Farmer and Smakman name this risk directly: **concentration of power**. A society that routes billions of daily decisions through a few firms' assistants has not decentralized choice; it has centralized it behind a friendlier interface. Hold onto that thought. It returns at institutional scale in the next section, and at political scale in the last one.
