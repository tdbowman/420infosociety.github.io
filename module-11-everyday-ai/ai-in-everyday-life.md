# Module 11: AI and Automation in Everyday Life 🤝

**Week 11 | The Delegation Society**

## Welcome

Somewhere between the spellchecker and the software agent that books your flights, something important changed: you stopped *using* a tool and started *delegating* a task. This week we examine that shift at home, at work, and in some of the highest-stakes institutions in the country. Our question is not whether delegation is convenient (it obviously is), but what it quietly rearranges: your skills, your relationships, your accountability, and your power.

### 🎯 What You'll Learn

- The spectrum from tool to assistant to adviser to autonomous agent, and why each step changes who is responsible for the outcome
- The Ada Lovelace Institute's framework for AI assistants as *executors*, *advisers*, and *interlocutors*, and the risks it flags: dependency, concentration of power, and blurred accountability
- How AI adoption inside workplaces can erode the implicit "psychological contract" between employers and employees, following Braganza and colleagues
- Why institutions drowning in volume, like an Air Force facing millions of daily cybersecurity alerts, reach for AI as a triage layer for scarce human attention
- How current U.S. AI policy frames everyday AI, how the previous administration framed it differently, and what an ordinary user can actually influence

### 🧠 Big Questions to Consider

- When your agent makes a bad call on your behalf, who owes the apology? You, the agent, or the company that built it?
- What skills are you comfortable letting atrophy, and which ones do you refuse to hand over?
- If nearly everyone's personal agent is built by one of a handful of companies, who is really being represented in your negotiations?
- Why does the same public that uses AI daily report more concern than excitement about it?
- Is "humans handle judgment, machines handle volume" a stable division of labor, or just the current position of a moving line?

## This Week's Plan

We move from the personal to the institutional to the political. First, we map the delegation spectrum and ask what changes when software acts *for* you rather than *with* you. Second, we follow AI into two very different institutions, the workplace and the military, and find the same pattern in both: machines absorb volume so humans can concentrate judgment. Third, we look at how the United States is trying to steer all of this, and what practical AI literacy looks like for someone who cannot write an executive order.
