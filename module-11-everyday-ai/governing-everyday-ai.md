## Two Blueprints for the Same Technology

In July 2025, the White House released *Winning the Race: America's AI Action Plan*, the Trump administration's blueprint for national AI policy. Its three pillars are visible in its table of contents: accelerating AI innovation, building American AI infrastructure (chips, data centers, and the energy to power them), and leading internationally in AI diplomacy and security. The plan's organizing metaphor is right there in the title: a race. Its recurring worry is that regulation, red tape, and ideological requirements could slow American AI down while competitors, above all China, speed up.

The previous administration ran a different play. The Biden-era approach, summarized in the archived 2024 White House fact sheet on the readings list, centered on a sweeping 2023 executive order on "safe, secure, and trustworthy" AI: safety-test reporting for the most powerful models, guidance to manage AI's risks in workplaces and consumer products, attention to discrimination and privacy, and a companion "AI Bill of Rights" framing that put individual protections in the foreground. The organizing metaphor was less a race than an inspection: innovation, yes, but with guardrails bolted on first.

Set side by side, and this course asks you to do it neutrally, as an analyst rather than a partisan, the two documents are a compact lesson in how framing operates:

| | AI Action Plan (2025) | Biden-era approach (2023–24) |
|---|---|---|
| Core metaphor | A race to win | A powerful technology to make trustworthy |
| Primary risk feared | Falling behind rivals; overregulation | Harms to safety, rights, and fairness |
| Main instruments | Deregulation, infrastructure buildout, exports and diplomacy | Executive order, agency guidance, testing and reporting requirements |
| Everyday-AI focus | Adoption: getting AI into workplaces, government, and the economy | Protection: managing AI's effects on workers and consumers |

Neither document is indifferent to the other's concern. The Action Plan discusses security and worker impacts; the executive order promoted innovation. But each decides which concern gets to be the headline. Recall the Farmer and Smakman briefing from Section 1: its worries were dependency, concentrated power, and accountability. Those worries fare very differently under each blueprint. Notice, too, what *both* blueprints treat as secondary: the texture of everyday delegation. Your agent, your inbox, your boss's scoring system. That texture is shaped less by any national plan than by product defaults set in a handful of corporate headquarters.

:::{note} Policy has a refresh rate
A caution before you memorize the table: AI policy now changes faster than textbooks do. Executive-branch documents can be rescinded or replaced with each administration. The 2023 executive order itself was revoked in early 2025, which is why its fact sheet lives on an archive site. Treat the specifics here as a snapshot from mid-2026, and treat the *comparison method* (find the metaphor, find the feared risk, find the instrument) as the durable skill. That method will still work on whatever plan is current when you read this.
:::

## What You Can and Can't Influence

It is easy to read national strategies and conclude that everyday users are spectators. That is roughly half true, and the honest half matters.

What you mostly *cannot* influence: the infrastructure buildout, the export rules, which frontier models get trained, and the default settings of platforms with billions of users. These are decided by capital expenditure, procurement, and executive action. The Pew survey's finding of concern outrunning excitement has not, by itself, redirected any of them. Public sentiment is a slow, blunt instrument.

What you *can* influence is smaller but real. You can comment: federal agencies implementing AI policy run public comment periods, and state legislatures (currently the most active AI lawmakers in the country) hold hearings where ordinary testimony is read. You can choose: delegation, unlike infrastructure, still happens one checkbox at a time, and products do respond when users conspicuously refuse a feature. You can negotiate where you have standing: workplace AI, meaning the algorithmic management and monitoring from Section 2, is a condition of employment, which makes it a subject for the same channels workers have always used, from direct questions ("what does the system log, and who reviews it?") to collective bargaining. And you can set norms. Most of the etiquette of everyday AI, whether you disclose the agent and whether the machine-written note counts, is being written right now, informally, by people your age.

## Practical AI Literacy: Disclosure, Verification, and the Do-Not-Delegate List

Which brings us to the most usable thing this module can teach. If policy sets the defaults and companies set the products, your remaining sovereignty is *practice*: the habits you bring to daily delegation. Three of them matter most.

**Disclosure.** Tell people when an agent acted for you, in any context where they would feel deceived to learn it later. The test is not "is it technically my message?" but "would the relationship survive the reveal?" Recall from Module 3 that people judge senders more harshly when they merely *suspect* undisclosed AI help. The delegation society raises those stakes, because now it is your presence, not just your phrasing, that may be synthetic. Disclosure norms are cheap for you and valuable for everyone: they keep the difference between a person and a person's agent visible while that difference still matters.

**Verification.** An agent's report is a claim, not a receipt. The habit costs minutes: check the confirmation email yourself, skim what was sent in your name, spot-check the summary against one original source. Its absence is how small errors compound in the dark. Section 2's lesson about deference applies to you too: the human-in-the-loop who never checks is a signature, not a safeguard.

**Knowing when not to delegate.** This is the sophisticated skill, and it is a deliberate personal policy rather than either maximal delegation or blanket refusal. Four kinds of tasks earn a place on a do-not-delegate list. Tasks that are *irreversible or high-stakes*: anything you could not undo or would struggle to explain. Tasks that are *value-laden*: decisions that encode who you are, which no goal-specification captures. Tasks that *are the relationship*: the condolence note, the apology, the recommendation letter, which is Section 1's point that some chores are the caring. And tasks that *train you*: the struggle you are tempted to outsource in college is often the curriculum itself. Delegate the first draft and you may be automating your own apprenticeship, the personal version of the pipeline erosion from Section 2.

:::{tip} Write the list down
Before Friday, actually draft your do-not-delegate list: five items, one sentence of reasoning each. Vague intentions lose to convenient defaults every time, and a written policy at least forces the checkbox to argue with you. Compare lists in class. The disagreements (is dating-app conversation delegable? a scholarship essay outline?) are where the interesting sociology lives.
:::

## Setting Up Your Futures Portfolio

One piece of housekeeping with intellectual content. Starting this week, open a document called your Futures Portfolio. In it, write down three predictions, each one sentence: how you expect AI delegation to change, over the next five years, (1) your own likely occupation, (2) one relationship in your life, and (3) one institution you depend on. Date them. Note, for each, what evidence would change your mind.

Why now? This module gave you frameworks and sentiment (the delegation spectrum, the psychological contract, a public more concerned than excited) but deliberately light evidence about the biggest question hanging over all of it, namely jobs. That is by design. Module 12 supplies the labor-market evidence: what economists actually measure about AI, automation, and work, which is messier and more interesting than either the doom or the hype. Your portfolio predictions, made *before* you see that evidence, are the experiment. You will revisit and grade them at the end of the course.
