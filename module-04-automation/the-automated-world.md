## Your Morning, Automated

Before you spoke to another human being this morning, machines had already made a remarkable number of decisions on your behalf. A thermostat sensed the temperature and decided whether to heat your room. Your phone decided which notifications deserved to wake the screen. If you bought coffee, a point-of-sale system decided whether your card was likely fraudulent, in milliseconds, without asking anyone. If you took a bus or an elevator, machines managed the schedule and the descent. None of this felt like "automation." It felt like nothing at all — which is precisely the point. Automation that works disappears into the background of life, and we reserve the word for whatever machines have *just started* doing, usually the thing making headlines.

That habit makes us bad historians. Automation — machines carrying out tasks with reduced human intervention — is not a twenty-first-century invention, or even a twentieth-century one. In 1804, the Jacquard loom used chains of punched cards to weave intricate patterns automatically, a technology so suggestive that early computing pioneers borrowed its cards. Ford's moving assembly line, from 1913, reorganized human work around the rhythm of machines. The humble thermostat, sensing and responding without any person in the loop, is a fully automated control system that predates your grandparents. Whatever is new about our moment, it is not the *idea* of machines doing things by themselves.

It helps to draw one old distinction first. *Mechanization* replaces human muscle: a power loom weaves faster than hands, but a person still tends it, decides, corrects. *Automation* replaces human control: the machine senses its situation and acts on its own. A tractor mechanizes plowing; a thermostat automates heating. Most of the anxiety in this module lives on the automation side of that line — because control, judgment, and decision were supposed to be the human contribution.

## The Spectrum, from Looms to Humanoids

Rather than asking whether something "is automated," ask *how* automated it is, and in what way. The figure below lays out the spectrum this module will use.

:::{figure} images/automation-spectrum.svg
:name: automation-spectrum
:alt: A horizontal diagram of five labeled boxes connected by rightward arrows, showing the automation spectrum from mechanization, through fixed automation, programmable automation, and adaptive AI-driven automation, to embodied autonomy in humanoid form, with a one-line example under each stage and a long arrow beneath the boxes running from human-guided machines on the left to self-guided machines on the right.
:width: 100%

The automation spectrum. Each stage adds machine capability — but none fully retires the previous ones, which is why all five still surround you today.
:::

*Mechanization* amplifies human effort while keeping a person in charge. *Fixed automation* commits a machine to one task — a bottling line fills bottles, superbly and forever, and can do nothing else. *Programmable automation* adds flexibility: the same robot arm that welded last year's car model can be reprogrammed for this year's. *Adaptive, AI-driven automation* adds perception and adjustment: warehouse robots that sense obstacles, learn layouts, and reroute themselves are not following a fixed script. And at the far right sits *embodied autonomy* — humanoid robots meant to operate in spaces built for human bodies — which is the newest, loudest, and by far the least proven stage. Next section, we'll watch one learn factory work and ask hard questions about it.

Two things about this spectrum matter. First, it is cumulative, not sequential: the world did not upgrade from looms to humanoids. Your city contains all five stages at once, and the middle of the spectrum — fixed and programmable automation — still does the overwhelming share of the world's automated work. Second, movement rightward along the spectrum is exactly where AI enters the story. What distinguishes the adaptive stage from the programmable one is not better motors but better *control* — software that perceives, predicts, and decides. Automation is the body; AI is increasingly the nervous system.

## What Is Actually New: Scope

If automation is two centuries old, why does this moment feel different? Because the *scope* has exploded along three fronts at once.

First, automation left the factory. For most of its history, serious automation lived behind fences, in purpose-built industrial spaces. Now it operates in public: warehouse robotics fulfilling your orders overnight, self-checkout kiosks turning you into the cashier, delivery robots on sidewalks. You no longer visit the automated world on a field trip; you live in it.

Second, automation became software. A machine does not need a body to automate work. Code that processes insurance claims, screens résumés, flags transactions, or drafts emails is automation in every meaningful sense — it takes over tasks people were paid to do — and it spreads at the speed of a download, not a factory retrofit. This is why the AI wave feels categorically different from the robot wave: software automation has no natural habitat and no fences.

Third, and least visible: automation began managing *people*. Under what researchers call *algorithmic management*, software assigns the work, sets the pace, monitors performance, and sometimes decides who keeps working at all. Ride-hail drivers are dispatched, priced, rated, and deactivated by systems; warehouse workers pick to the tempo of a scanner. Notice the inversion, because it is the sharpest twist in this module: the classic automation story is machines replacing workers, but here the machine took over the *boss's* tasks, and the humans stayed — working for an algorithm. Ask the course's standing question: who benefits from that arrangement, and who would even hear an appeal against it?

## Tasks, Not Jobs

Now for the distinction that will do more work for you than any other this week — in the readings, in the news, and in every argument you will ever have about automation. Machines automate *tasks*. People hold *jobs*. And a job is not one task; it is a bundle of them, wrapped in judgment, relationships, exceptions, and responsibility.

Consider the bank teller, an example we will revisit in Section 3. Counting out cash is a task, and machines took it over decades ago. But the teller's job also contained verifying identities, catching confusion and fraud, calming upset customers, and selling the bank's services — tasks that survived, and around which the job reorganized. Or consider the work you're training for now: whatever it is, it is a bundle, and some of its tasks are almost certainly automatable while others stubbornly are not. The interesting question is never "will a machine take the job?" but "which tasks go, which remain, what new ones appear — and does the reorganized bundle still add up to a livelihood, at what pay, for whom?"

:::{warning} When You Hear "X% of Jobs Will Be Automated"
Nearly every alarming statistic in this genre is a *task-share estimate* — some fraction of the tasks inside various jobs looks technically automatable — that gets translated by headline into *job losses*. Those are wildly different claims. A job can lose a third of its tasks and become more valuable; it can also survive intact but at lower pay, or split into a good job and a bad one. Whenever a number like this appears, ask the course's question: which definition of "automated" is doing the work in this claim, and who benefits from the scarier reading?
:::

This is also where this week's readings plug in. Ivanov, Kuyumdzhiev, and Webster will show you that people's *fear* of automation tracks how vulnerable they believe their own bundle of tasks to be — beliefs formed less by task-level analysis than by stories. And the Budget Lab at Yale will show you what happens when you go looking for automation's effects in actual labor-market data rather than in predictions. Before we can weigh either, though, we need to see what the machines can really do. Time to visit the factory floor.
