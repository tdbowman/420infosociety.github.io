# Module 4: Automation and Robotics 🦾

**Week 4 | Machines That Move, and What They Move Aside**

## Welcome

Last week we met AI as a talker — fluent, impressive, unreliable. This week we meet machines that *act*: robot arms that weld, warehouse fleets that never clock out, humanoids learning factory work in a body shaped like yours. Our job is to untangle three things: what automation has always been, what is genuinely new about it, and what the evidence actually says about who gets moved aside.

### 🎯 What You'll Learn

- Why automation is centuries older than AI — and what really *is* new about this wave: its scope, from warehouses to software to the management of workers themselves
- The most useful distinction in every automation debate: machines automate *tasks*, but people hold *jobs*
- Where robots actually work today (the IFR's latest global numbers), and why structured environments are their natural habitat
- What humanoid robots can and can't do yet, and why a stunning demo is not a working deployment
- What drives fear of automation, and what careful analysis of the labor market shows so far

### 🧠 Big Questions to Consider

- If a machine takes over a third of your tasks, has it taken a third of your job — or changed what your job is?
- Why do robots that backflip on video still struggle to fold laundry?
- Two hundred years of automation panic have not produced mass permanent unemployment. Does that history predict the AI wave — or lull us into missing what's different this time?
- Who benefits when the story is "the robots are coming for everyone" — and who benefits when the story is "relax, it's all hype"?

## This Week's Journey

We move from the familiar to the contested. First, we take inventory of the automated world you already live in and build a vocabulary for talking about it precisely — including the automation spectrum, from the power loom to the humanoid. Second, we go to the factory floor: where robots thrive, where they stall, and what the humanoid moment is really made of. Third, we turn to the fear itself: what drives it, how old it is, and what the current evidence does and doesn't show.
