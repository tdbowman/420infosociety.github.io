## Two Hundred Years of the Same Headline

In the 1810s, skilled English textile workers calling themselves Luddites smashed the knitting frames and power looms spreading through their trade. History remembers them as technophobes — "Luddite" survives as an insult for anyone who irrationally hates technology. The record is more interesting: the Luddites were craftsmen at the top of their trade, and their grievance was less the machines than *how* they were deployed — to replace skilled work with cheap labor, cutting the craftsmen out of the gains. They were not arguing with the loom. They were arguing with the loom's owners about who would be counted as necessary. Two centuries later, that is still the argument.

The anxiety has returned with every wave since. In 1930, the economist John Maynard Keynes coined the term *technological unemployment* — joblessness caused by machines displacing workers faster than new uses for labor appear — and predicted his readers would be hearing a great deal about it. They did. In the 1960s, the automation scare around computers and industrial machinery grew serious enough that a U.S. president convened a national commission to study whether automation was about to sever the link between machines and jobs. (Its broad conclusion: the machines were changing work, but the mass unemployment was not materializing.) And in the decades that followed, the ATM became the textbook case: everyone knew the cash machine would eliminate the bank teller, yet teller work persisted for decades, reorganizing around the tasks machines couldn't do — service, judgment, sales — even as counting cash ceased to be a human job. You built this expectation yourself in Section 1: machines took *tasks*; the *job* changed shape.

Notice what this history teaches and what it does not. Across two centuries, the recurring prophecy of permanent mass unemployment has recurringly failed: new tasks, industries, and jobs appeared, and societies got richer. But the reassuring aggregate hides brutal particulars. Transitions destroyed specific livelihoods, devalued specific skills, and hollowed out specific towns — and the people who absorbed those costs were rarely the people who captured the gains. The honest lesson of automation history is double: the *economy* has always adapted, and *individual people* have often been wrecked while it did. Both halves are true, and every automation debate you will ever hear consists of people shouting one half at people shouting the other.

## What Drives the Fear

Whatever the machines end up doing, the fear is already here — and it turns out fear is a researchable object. This week's Ivanov, Kuyumdzhiev, and Webster reading treats automation anxiety not as a mood to editorialize about but as a variable to measure: their survey research asks who fears automation, and why.

The headline finding is intuitive once you see it: fear tracks *perceived* vulnerability. People who believe their own job's tasks could be taken over by machines fear automation; people who feel irreplaceable, or confident they could retrain, largely don't. The load-bearing word is *perceived*. The fear runs through beliefs about one's job — beliefs formed by headlines, demos, and conversation at least as much as by any task-by-task analysis of the kind you did in Section 1. Which means the fear can decouple from the facts in either direction: a viral humanoid video can terrify workers whose jobs are decades from automatable, while workers whose task bundles are quietly being eaten by software may feel no alarm at all, because nothing about their week looks like science fiction.

And misplaced or not, the fear itself has costs. People who expect their occupation to vanish plan differently — what they study, what they buy, whether they speak up at work — and a workforce marinating in automation dread pays those costs even in futures where the robots never arrive. That is why Ivanov and colleagues pair drivers with *solutions* running through both individuals and institutions: education and retraining that offer realistic paths toward less-automatable work, and public policy sturdy enough to catch those whom the transition actually displaces. You needn't endorse their particular list to accept the underlying point — fear of automation is not weather; it responds to what schools, employers, and governments visibly do.

## What the Evidence Shows (So Far)

So the fear is measurable and the history is long. What about the present? Since Module 3's ChatGPT moment, predictions of imminent, sweeping AI-driven job destruction have been a fixture of the discourse. This is a factual claim, and it can be checked against data.

The Budget Lab at Yale checked. Their analysis, published in late 2025, went looking for AI's fingerprints in the U.S. labor market — examining, among other things, whether the mix of occupations has been churning faster or differently since generative AI arrived than in earlier periods of technological change. Their finding, stated with appropriate care: no detectable economy-wide, AI-driven disruption of employment yet. The occupational structure was shifting at rates broadly in line with past technological transitions — the ordinary metabolism of a changing economy, not a rupture. Three years into the most-hyped technology of the century, the aggregate labor-market data did not show the robot apocalypse.

Sit with how strange and useful that finding is. It does not say AI is having no effects — individual firms, tasks, and workers were plainly being affected. It says the effects had not, by late 2025, added up to anything distinguishable from normal change in the economy-wide statistics. Between the demo and the deployment, between the announcement and the aggregate, stands everything Section 2 taught you: integration costs, reliability gaps, reorganization, and time. Hold this study up against every "AI has already destroyed the job market" headline you have seen, and practice the course's reflex: what would count as evidence for the claim, and does the claimant have any?

## The Weight of "Yet"

And yet — *yet*. Every sentence in that Yale finding ends with an implicit "as of late 2025," and intellectual honesty requires noticing how much weight that qualifier carries. There are at least three reasons the absence of disruption so far settles less than it seems to.

First, technology's labor effects arrive on a lag. Electrification took decades to reshape factories, because capturing the gains required rebuilding processes and organizations, not just installing motors. If foundation models follow that pattern, the aggregate statistics of the mid-2020s would look calm even if large changes were coming. Second, aggregates can stay smooth while the composition underneath churns: economy-wide measures can miss effects concentrated in particular occupations, regions, or — as much current debate wonders — entry-level work, where a decision not to hire is exactly the kind of quiet non-event that never makes a statistic scream. Third, capability is still moving; Section 2's gatekeepers — reliability, dexterity, cost — are engineering problems, and engineering problems have a way of eventually being solved.

None of this licenses flipping back to panic. It means both cheap positions are unearned: "no disruption yet, therefore never" ignores the lags, and "disruption is inevitable, the data just hasn't caught up" is unfalsifiable prophecy. The defensible stance is the uncomfortable one — watch the evidence, know what would change your mind, and remember from the Luddites forward that the real fight has never been machines versus humans, but over how the gains and losses get shared.

So carry this question forward, because it is the one this module cannot answer and Module 12, on the future of work, will take up directly: *if* the disruption comes, what determines whether it plays out as the ATM story — tasks absorbed, jobs reshaped, gains eventually shared — or as the Luddites' story, where the people displaced were simply left out of the settlement? The machines will not decide that. People, institutions, and power will — which is why it belongs in a course on the information *society*.
