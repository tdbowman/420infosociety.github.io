## Two Hundred Years of the Same Headline

In the 1810s, skilled English textile workers calling themselves Luddites smashed the knitting frames and power looms spreading through their trade. History remembers them as technophobes, and "Luddite" survives as an insult for anyone who irrationally hates technology. The record is more interesting. The Luddites were craftsmen at the top of their trade, and their grievance was less the machines than *how* they were deployed: to replace skilled work with cheap labor, cutting the craftsmen out of the gains. They were not arguing with the loom. They were arguing with the loom's owners about who would be counted as necessary. Two centuries later, that is still the argument.

The anxiety has returned with every wave since. In 1930, the economist John Maynard Keynes coined the term **technological unemployment**, meaning joblessness caused by machines displacing workers faster than new uses for labor appear, and predicted his readers would be hearing a great deal about it. They did. In the 1960s, the automation scare around computers and industrial machinery grew serious enough that a U.S. president convened a national commission to study whether automation was about to sever the link between machines and jobs. (Its broad conclusion: the machines were changing work, but the mass unemployment was not materializing.) In the decades that followed, the ATM became the textbook case. Everyone knew the cash machine would eliminate the bank teller, yet teller work persisted for decades, reorganizing around the tasks machines couldn't do (service, judgment, sales) even as counting cash stopped being a human job. You built this expectation yourself in Section 1: machines took *tasks*, and the *job* changed shape.

Be careful about what this history teaches and what it doesn't. Across two centuries, the recurring prophecy of permanent mass unemployment has recurringly failed. New tasks, industries, and jobs appeared, and societies got richer. But the reassuring aggregate hides brutal particulars. Transitions destroyed specific livelihoods, devalued specific skills, and hollowed out specific towns, and the people who absorbed those costs were rarely the people who captured the gains. The honest lesson of automation history is double. The *economy* has always adapted, and *individual people* have often been wrecked while it did. Both halves are true, and every automation debate you will ever hear consists of people shouting one half at people shouting the other.

## What Drives the Fear

Whatever the machines end up doing, the fear is already here, and fear turns out to be a researchable object. This week's Ivanov, Kuyumdzhiev, and Webster reading treats automation anxiety as a variable to measure rather than a mood to editorialize about. Their survey research asks who fears automation, and why.

The headline finding is intuitive once you see it: fear tracks *perceived* vulnerability. People who believe their own job's tasks could be taken over by machines fear automation. People who feel irreplaceable, or confident they could retrain, largely don't. Pay attention to that word *perceived*. The fear runs through beliefs about one's job, and those beliefs come from headlines, demos, and conversation at least as much as from any task-by-task analysis of the kind you did in Section 1. So the fear can come apart from the facts in either direction. A viral humanoid video can terrify workers whose jobs are decades from automatable, while workers whose task bundles are quietly being eaten by software may feel no alarm at all, because nothing about their week looks like science fiction.

Misplaced or not, the fear itself has costs. People who expect their occupation to vanish plan differently: what they study, what they buy, whether they speak up at work. A workforce marinating in automation dread pays those costs even in futures where the robots never arrive. That is why Ivanov and colleagues pair drivers with *solutions* aimed at both individuals and institutions. Education and retraining that offer realistic paths toward less-automatable work, plus public policy sturdy enough to catch the people the transition actually displaces. You needn't endorse their particular list to accept the underlying point. Fear of automation is not weather. It responds to what schools, employers, and governments visibly do.

## What the Evidence Shows (So Far)

So the fear is measurable and the history is long. What about the present? Since Module 3's ChatGPT moment, predictions of imminent, sweeping AI-driven job destruction have been everywhere. That is a factual claim, and it can be checked against data.

The Budget Lab at Yale checked. Their analysis, published in late 2025, went looking for AI's fingerprints in the U.S. labor market, examining among other things whether the mix of occupations has been churning faster or differently since generative AI arrived than in earlier periods of technological change. Their finding, stated with appropriate care: no detectable economy-wide, AI-driven disruption of employment yet. The occupational structure was shifting at rates broadly in line with past technological transitions, which is the ordinary metabolism of a changing economy rather than a rupture. Three years into the most-hyped technology of the century, the aggregate labor-market data did not show the robot apocalypse.

Sit with how strange and useful that finding is. It does not say AI is having no effects; individual firms, tasks, and workers were plainly being affected. It says the effects had not, by late 2025, added up to anything you could distinguish from normal change in the economy-wide statistics. Between the announcement and the aggregate stands everything Section 2 taught you: integration costs, reliability gaps, reorganization, and time. Hold this study up against every "AI has already destroyed the job market" headline you have seen, and practice the course's reflex. What would count as evidence for the claim, and does the claimant have any?

## The Weight of "Yet"

Every sentence in that Yale finding ends with an implicit "as of late 2025," and intellectual honesty requires noticing how much that qualifier carries. There are at least three reasons the absence of disruption so far settles less than it seems to.

First, technology's labor effects arrive on a lag. Electrification took decades to reshape factories, because capturing the gains required rebuilding processes and organizations, not just installing motors. If foundation models follow that pattern, the aggregate statistics of the mid-2020s would look calm even if large changes were coming. Second, aggregates can stay smooth while the composition underneath churns. Economy-wide measures can miss effects concentrated in particular occupations, particular regions, or entry-level work, which is where much of the current debate is focused; a decision not to hire is exactly the kind of quiet non-event that never makes a statistic scream. Third, capability is still moving. Section 2's gatekeepers of reliability, dexterity, and cost are engineering problems, and engineering problems have a way of eventually being solved.

None of this licenses flipping back to panic. It means both cheap positions are unearned. "No disruption yet, therefore never" ignores the lags. "Disruption is inevitable, the data just hasn't caught up" is unfalsifiable prophecy. The defensible stance is the uncomfortable one: watch the evidence, know what would change your mind, and remember, from the Luddites forward, that the real fight has never been machines versus humans. It has been about how the gains and losses get shared.

So carry this question forward. It is the one this module cannot answer and Module 12, on the future of work, will take up directly. *If* the disruption comes, what determines whether it plays out like the ATM story, with tasks absorbed, jobs reshaped, and gains eventually shared, or like the Luddites' story, where the people displaced were simply left out of the settlement? The machines will not decide that. People, institutions, and power will, which is why it belongs in a course on the information *society*.
