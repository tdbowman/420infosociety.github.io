## When the Volunteers Log On

A war breaks out on the other side of the world. You are a twenty-year-old with a laptop and strong feelings about it. Within hours, you find a channel where organizers post target lists — government sites, banks, a railway booking system in the enemy country — along with simple tools and instructions. Join in, and by dinnertime you have participated in an attack on another nation's infrastructure, from your dorm, without joining any army, wearing any uniform, or asking anyone's permission.

This is not hypothetical; it has become a standard feature of twenty-first-century conflicts, with civilian hacker groups on multiple sides claiming attacks and coordinating openly on social platforms. And it collides head-on with a body of law built over centuries around a very different picture of war. **International humanitarian law** (IHL) — the laws of war — rests on distinctions: combatant versus civilian, military target versus civilian object, proportionate force versus wanton destruction. Those distinctions assume you can tell who is fighting, and that the fighters are organized, identifiable, and answerable to a command structure that can be held responsible. A swarm of anonymous volunteers attacking a railway's ticketing system fits none of that. The uncomfortable question of this section: do the old rules stretch to cover the new battlefield, or do we need new rules — and who has the standing to write them?

## Borrowing From the Bomb

When faced with a genuinely new weapon, ethicists reach for the last genuinely new weapon. This week's Wilson and Fitz reading works through the most tempting analogy: nuclear weapons, the technology that forced the twentieth century to rebuild its thinking about deterrence, escalation, and what may never be targeted. How well does the analogy hold?

Some things transfer. The core moral vocabulary of nuclear ethics — **proportionality** (a response must not be wildly out of scale with the attack), civilian protection (some targets are off-limits no matter what), escalation risk (small exchanges can spiral) — applies to cyber operations with barely any translation. An attack that shuts down a power grid in winter raises exactly the questions nuclear ethicists spent decades sharpening: who suffers, how much, and could the attacker have achieved the military aim another way?

But Wilson and Fitz's more useful contribution is the list of places the analogy breaks, because each break is a hole in our ethical machinery.

| Dimension | Nuclear weapons | Cyber weapons |
|---|---|---|
| Attribution | Missiles have return addresses; launch is unambiguous | Attacks can be routed, disguised, and false-flagged; attribution takes weeks and stays contestable |
| Who has them | A handful of states, at enormous cost | States, gangs, and volunteers; capabilities cheap and copyable |
| Reversibility | Effects are absolute and permanent | Effects range from a rebooted server to destroyed infrastructure — often unclear even to the attacker |
| Thresholds | Use is a bright, visible line no one has crossed since 1945 | Constant low-level intrusion; no agreed line where "attack" becomes "act of war" |
| Deterrence | Stable(ish): retaliation is certain and catastrophic | Shaky: you can't retaliate against an attacker you can't identify |

Look at the first row longest. Nuclear deterrence worked — to the extent it worked — because attribution was trivial: everyone knew where the missile came from, so retaliation was credible. Cyber attribution is slow, technical, and deniable, which means the entire deterrence logic inherited from the Cold War wobbles. A state can attack while denying it; a state can be *framed*; and a defender may face pressure to retaliate before the evidence is in. Notice, too, that the analogy fails at the entry gate: building a bomb takes a national program, while capable cyber tools leak, get stolen, and get copied. Nuclear ethics never had to ask what happens when individuals hold weapons of statecraft. Cyber ethics asks it daily — which brings us back to our volunteer with the laptop.

## Eight Rules Nobody Asked For

In October 2023, the International Committee of the Red Cross — the organization that guards the Geneva Conventions — did something remarkable: it published eight rules directly addressed to "civilian hackers" participating in armed conflicts, along with four obligations for the states that encourage them. The rules will sound familiar if you've absorbed anything about IHL: do not attack civilian objects; do not use malware that spreads indiscriminately and damages military and civilian targets alike; hospitals are never targets; do not threaten violence to spread terror among civilians; comply even if your enemy does not.

Pause on what it means that this document *needed to exist*. The ICRC talks to soldiers because soldiers are organized and trained and their states are bound by treaty. Civilian hacktivists are none of those things — so the Red Cross found itself doing something closer to public outreach, essentially posting the Geneva Conventions where the volunteers hang out and hoping. The accompanying message to states — you have obligations to restrain "your" hacktivists — acknowledges the open secret that many of these groups operate with the tolerance, and sometimes the quiet encouragement, of governments that value the deniability.

The response proved the point. The ICRC reported pushback from hacktivist groups on multiple sides — some rejecting the rules outright, arguing variously that their cause justified their targets, that following rules would put them at a disadvantage, or that the ICRC had no authority over them anyway. Others were more receptive. The episode is a live experiment in a question this course keeps circling: can norms govern behavior when the actors are anonymous, distributed, and answer to no institution? The hacktivists who refused weren't merely being defiant; they were pointing at the enforcement hole. A soldier who violates IHL can face a war-crimes tribunal. What exactly happens to an anonymous volunteer who takes down a hospital's scheduling system from a country the victim can't even identify?

:::{note} Norms Before Enforcement
It's tempting to dismiss unenforceable rules as empty. History suggests otherwise: most of IHL began as norms that states signed long before meaningful enforcement existed, and the norms shaped behavior anyway — imperfectly, but measurably. The ICRC is betting the same slow mechanism can work on hackers. Whether norms can bind people who never signed anything is one of the questions your generation of information professionals will answer, one way or the other.
:::

## The Gray Zone Below War

There's one more complication, and you've already met it. Back in Module 5 we examined Salt Typhoon, the sprawling state-linked espionage campaign that burrowed into telecommunications networks and sat there, reading. Was that war? Almost certainly not — espionage is ancient, and international law has always given it a strange, tolerated non-status. But an intruder deep inside civilian infrastructure is not just *reading*: the access that lets you listen today can let you destroy tomorrow. Security analysts call this pre-positioning, and it lives in the gap Wilson and Fitz identified — below any agreed threshold of "attack," yet miles beyond anything we'd tolerate physically. Imagine foreign soldiers quietly living in the basement of every phone company in the country, insisting they're only there to take notes.

This gray zone, not the dramatic cyber-Pearl-Harbor scenario, is where most cyber conflict actually happens: constant intrusion, ambiguous intent, deniable authorship — and, increasingly, AI on both ends, machine-generated lures getting intruders in and machine-speed monitoring hunting them once inside. The laws of war activate at a threshold that cyber operations are carefully engineered never to visibly cross. Which leaves defenders, governments, and companies making ethical decisions in territory the rulebooks don't reach — exactly where we're headed next.
