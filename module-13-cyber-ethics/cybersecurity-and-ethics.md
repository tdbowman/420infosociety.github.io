# Module 13: Cybersecurity and Ethics 🎩

**Week 13 | White Hats, Gray Zones**

## Welcome

Somewhere right now, a security researcher is staring at a flaw in software you use and deciding what to do about it — tell the company, tell the world, sell it, or walk away. This week we treat cybersecurity not as a technical subject but as an ethical one: a field of people with dangerous skills, weak professional guardrails, and decisions that affect millions of strangers. From bug-hunters to wartime hacktivists to boardrooms weighing a ransom, the question is the same — what should you do when you have the power to break things?

### 🎯 What You'll Learn

- What actually separates white-hat, gray-hat, and black-hat hackers — and why the categories blur in practice
- Why authorization is the bright line in security work, and how vulnerability disclosure became one of tech's longest-running ethical debates
- What nuclear-weapons ethics can and cannot teach us about cyber conflict, and why the Red Cross published rules for civilian hackers
- How the ethical frameworks from Module 6 perform under pressure in three real dilemmas: hacking back, paying ransoms, and AI-enabled deception
- Why cybersecurity practitioners face ethical decisions daily without anything like medicine's professional infrastructure

### 🧠 Big Questions to Consider

- If breaking into a system without permission is always a crime, but sometimes the only way a flaw gets fixed, what should a researcher do?
- Should a company that has been hacked be allowed to hack back — and if not, who exactly is supposed to protect it?
- Can rules written for bombs and borders govern weapons made of code, launched by volunteers from their couches?
- When a hospital's systems are encrypted and patients are at risk, is paying the ransom a moral failure or a moral obligation?
- If an AI can convincingly impersonate your boss on a video call, what does "verify before you trust" even mean anymore?

## This Week's Journey

We start inside the hacker world: hat colors, penetration testing, the disclosure dilemma, and what this week's Flechais and Chalhoub reading reveals about a profession that handles life-critical systems without a shared ethical code. Then we zoom out to war, using Wilson and Fitz's nuclear analogy and the Red Cross's controversial rules for civilian hackers. Finally, we put Module 6's ethical frameworks to work on three hard cases — hack back, ransom payments, and the Arup deepfake fraud — that resist every tidy answer.
