## The First Half in One Argument

Here's the semester so far, compressed:

1. **Modules 1–2** gave you the frame: the information society is a contested transformation, "information" is a contested concept, and which definition someone uses determines what they think should be done.
2. **Modules 3–4** introduced the machines: AI systems that produce information and robots that act on the world, impressive and uneven and dual-use.
3. **Module 5** showed the stakes: when everything runs on information systems, everything can be attacked through them.
4. **Modules 6–7** asked the human questions: what *should* these systems do (ethics), and when should we believe them (trust)?
5. **Module 8** put it all together in the hardest case: a society drowning in information-shaped content, fighting over what's true.

That's the loop from the very first page of this book. Technology shapes society, and society shapes technology back through ethics documents, laws, trust decisions, and countermeasures. The second half of the course (communication, everyday AI, work, cyber ethics, social media) runs the same loop through the places you actually live.

:::{tip} A one-hour review that actually works
Don't re-read the modules. Instead, for each module, write from memory: one core concept, one piece of evidence, and one question you'd still like answered. Then check the module overview pages to fill gaps. Recall practice beats re-reading. That's cognitive science, not a folk study tip.
:::

## Peer-Review Workshop 1 (Friday)

Bring a **complete draft** of your Case Analysis. Complete means it has all required sections and is within shouting distance of 1,800 words. A partial draft wastes your reviewers' time and your feedback opportunity.

### How it works

1. You'll be assigned **two classmates' drafts** through Canvas peer review.
2. Read each draft once straight through, then again with the **assignment rubric** (on Canvas, the same one Dr. Bowman grades with).
3. For each draft, answer three prompts:
   - Point to the **strongest paragraph** and say why it works
   - Find **one claim that needs a citation** or better evidence
   - Identify **one place the structure loses you**: where you had to re-read or got confused
4. Submit your reviews in Canvas by the end of the day.

### Why this isn't busywork

Applying the rubric to someone else's paper is the fastest way to learn what the rubric actually rewards. Most students report their *own* revision improves more from giving reviews than from receiving them. Substantive reviews also count toward your Engagement grade, and "substantive" means specific. "Great job!" helps no one. "Your ethical analysis names three frameworks and applies none of them" is a gift.

:::{note} Receiving feedback like a professional
You will disagree with some feedback you receive. Good; that disagreement is information too. You owe your reviewers consideration, not obedience. Your revision memo for the Futures Portfolio later this semester will ask you to explain what you changed *and what you chose not to change, and why*. Start practicing that judgment now.
:::
